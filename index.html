Devarim–dvorim wordplay

This article is about the Hebrew wordplay connecting Deuteronomy and bees. For the biblical book, see Book of Deuteronomy.

The Devarim–dvorim wordplay is a Hebrew linguistic and midrashic association between devarim (דברים, “words” or “matters”)—the Hebrew name of the Book of Deuteronomy—and dvorim, also vocalized devorim (דבורים, “bees”). The resemblance in sound and spelling supports traditional teachings that compare the words of Torah to bees: sweet as honey for those who receive them, and sharp as a sting for those who reject or misuse them.[1]

Modern English offers a coincidental parallel in the spelling bee, a phrase that likewise binds words to bees. There, however, the insect is the product of folk etymology: the “bee” of a spelling bee or quilting bee most likely derives from a dialect word for neighbourly help, later reanalysed as the name of the insect. The Hebrew pun and the English idiom thus arrive at the same pairing by opposite means—one as deliberate homily, the other as unwitting reinterpretation.[27]

    Linguistic background

    In Hebrew, davar (דבר) means “word,” “thing,” or “matter”; its plural is devarim (דברים). The book commonly called Deuteronomy in English is named in Hebrew for its opening phrase, Eleh ha-devarim (אלה הדברים)—“These are the words…”—introducing Moses’ farewell discourses to Israel. [2]

    In Modern Hebrew, speakers usually distinguish two everyday senses of English “word.” A countable lexical item—the unit one looks up in a dictionary or counts in a sentence—is typically milah (מילה), plural milim (מילים). By contrast, davar / devarim more often means a “thing,” “matter,” or “affair,” or else speech considered as content, message, or utterance (“what was said”), rather than as a bare vocabulary token. Thus one may ask how many milim are in a paragraph, but speak of the devarim of a speech, a book, or a negotiation. The biblical book title Devarim sits closer to this second sense: not a pile of dictionary words, but weighty spoken matters.[6]

    The distinction is a late redistribution of older vocabulary rather than an ancient one. Millah already appears in biblical poetry—“there is no word (millah) on my tongue that You do not know” (Psalm 139:4), “His word (millato) was on my tongue” (2 Samuel 23:2), and frequently in the speeches of Job—while biblical Aramaic uses millin for “words” (Daniel 7:25). In Talmudic Aramaic, conversely, milta (מילתא) commonly means “matter” or “thing,” covering much the same ground as Hebrew davar. Biblical prose narrative overwhelmingly prefers davar, which is one reason the book could be titled Devarim and not Milim.[7]

    Even so, the verbal root of speech remains d-b-r (ד־ב־ר). Modern Hebrew ledaber (לדבר), “to speak,” and related forms such as dibbur (דיבור), “speech,” keep the same consonants as davar / devarim. Milah / milim name the word as a linguistic unit; diber / ledaber name the act of speaking. The Devarim–dvorim wordplay therefore echoes a root still alive in ordinary talk about speech, even after modern usage has partly shifted the noun “word” toward milah. Traditional exegesis exploits the root’s biographical dimension: Moses had protested lo ish devarim anokhi, “I am not a man of words” (Exodus 4:10), and described himself as aral sefatayim, “uncircumcised of lips” (Exodus 6:12, 6:30), so that Devarim Rabbah 1:1 and Midrash Tanchuma (Devarim 1) present the book’s opening as the healing of his speech—the man of no devarim concluding his life with a book of them.[8] Homiletic writers add that Modern Hebrew milah is a homograph of milah, “circumcision” (from מול), a coincidence of spelling—etymologically unrelated—that moralists nonetheless read together as a discipline of the tongue.

    By contrast, dvora (דבורה) means “bee,” with the plural dvorim / devorim (דבורים). Although modern historical linguistics generally treats “word” and “bee” as separate lexical items, their near-homophony and shared consonant pattern make them productive material for midrashic wordplay (derash), which often privileges meaningful echo over strict etymology.[3]

    Rabbinic literature supplies an explicit warrant for interpreting by sound. The Babylonian Talmud repeatedly employs the formula al tikrei (“do not read X but Y”), as at Berakhot 64a, where banayikh (“your sons”) is reread as bonayikh (“your builders”), and it treats words as acronyms (notarikon) at Shabbat 105a. The thirty-two hermeneutical rules attributed to R. Eliezer ben Yose ha-Gelili catalogue such devices among the legitimate tools of exposition. Within that framework, hearing devarim as devorim is a recognized interpretive move rather than an error of reading.[9]

    Lexicographers differ on whether devorah belongs to the root ד־ב־ר at all. Some connect the bee to a sense “to lead, arrange, or order” (compare Arabic dabbara, “to manage”), which would make the ordered swarm a genuine relative of ordered speech; others treat “bee” as an independent lexical item. Standard reference works record both positions. A comparable set of consonants underlies midbar (“wilderness”), and Hosea 2:16 places the two together when God leads Israel into the midbar in order to “speak (ve-dibarti) to her heart”—a juxtaposition later preachers cite when linking speech, wilderness, and covenant.

    Midrashic interpretation

    Rabbinic and later Jewish teachers frequently liken Torah to honey and to the bee. On this reading, Moses’ devarim are imaginatively heard as dvorim: speech that nourishes and speech that wounds. The honey image draws on biblical praise of God’s words as sweeter than honey (e.g. Psalm 19:11; 119:103), while the sting warns that Torah’s demands can cut those who treat them lightly.[1]

    The double effect has a classical Talmudic formulation independent of the bee image. At Yoma 72b the Torah is said to become “an elixir of life” (sam chayim) for one who is worthy and “a deadly potion” (sam ha-mavet) for one who is not; a parallel discussion at Taanit 7a, which compares words of Torah to water, draws the same contrast between the fitting and the unfitting student. Sweetness and sting are therefore located in the recipient rather than in the text, which is precisely the moral the devarim/devorim pun is used to teach.[10]

    The sting also has a scriptural cognate in the goad. Ecclesiastes 12:11 declares that “the words of the sages are like goads” (divrei chakhamim ke-darvonot)—a phrase whose consonants echo ד־ב־ר—and Chagigah 3b expounds it as speech that pricks the hearer toward life and directs the wayward, much as the herdsman’s goad turns the animal. Read alongside the honey verses, the two images together give Torah’s words a mouth and a point.

    The honey side of the comparison is developed most fully in aggadah and in medieval custom. Song of Songs 4:11, “honey and milk are under your tongue,” is applied to Torah in Song of Songs Rabbah; Ezekiel eats the divine scroll and finds it “in my mouth as sweet as honey” (Ezekiel 3:3); and Sifrei Devarim 306, commenting on Moses’ own image—“may my teaching drop like rain” (Deuteronomy 32:2)—unfolds a chain of similes in which words nourish gently or beat down hard depending on their hearers. Medieval Ashkenazic sources, notably Machzor Vitry, describe initiating a child into study by having him lick honey from letters written on a tablet, making the sweetness of devarim a literal pedagogy.[11]

    Halakhic literature contributes a further twist. Bees are not kosher creatures, yet their honey is permitted; the Talmud at Bekhorot 7b explains that bees gather and store nectar rather than generating honey from their own substance, a ruling codified by Maimonides (Mishneh Torah, Forbidden Foods 3:3). Preachers have turned the technicality into homily: sweetness may pass intact through a stinging vessel, as reproof can carry blessing.[12]

    Commentators also develop secondary images: bees gather nectar from many flowers into one hive, as students gather many facets of Torah into coherent understanding; bees work collectively, as Israel receives Moses’ words as a community; and the bee’s product is both delicate and durable, as oral and written teaching preserve Deuteronomy’s charge across generations.

    In Deuteronomy (Devarim)

    Deuteronomy presents itself as a book of speech—Moses restating, interpreting, and urging covenantal commitment before Israel’s entry into the land. The Hebrew title therefore names not only a literary corpus but a mode of address: words that persuade, warn, remember, and bless. The devarim/dvorim pun intensifies that claim by casting those discourses as living, purposeful speech rather than inert text.

    Midrash Halakhah defines the genre of these devarim narrowly. Sifrei Devarim, Piska 1, followed by Rashi on Deuteronomy 1:1, reads eleh ha-devarim as divrei tokhachah, “words of rebuke,” and decodes the place-names of the opening verse as allusions to Israel’s failures, so that the words rebuke without naming the sins outright. The same sources note the phrase “to all Israel”: the rebuke was delivered before the entire people so that no one could later claim he would have answered it had he been present. Deuteronomy’s devarim are thus understood as public, pointed speech—an emphasis that makes the bee comparison apt.[13]

    Talmudic discussion of the book’s voice reinforces the point. The Bavli calls Deuteronomy Mishneh Torah (after Deuteronomy 17:18), and at Megillah 31b Abaye distinguishes the curses of Leviticus 26, spoken in the plural and uttered “from the mouth of the Almighty,” from those of Deuteronomy 28, framed in the singular and said by Moses mi-pi atzmo, “of his own accord.” Nachmanides, in his introduction to the book, treats it instead as prophecy restated for the generation entering the land, and later kabbalistic writers describe the arrangement as the divine presence speaking through Moses’ throat. Questions of authorship are taken up at Bava Batra 14b–15a and Menachot 30a, including the closing verses that report Moses’ death.[14] Later commentators outside the Jewish tradition raised the same question about the book’s opening: Adam Clarke held that its first five verses “do not appear to be the work of Moses, but were added probably either by Joshua or Ezra,” while glossing the verb of 1:5 in the same way as the midrash, as making the law “bare” or plain.[32]

    Deuteronomy 1:5 says that Moses “began to expound (be’er) this Torah”; Rashi, drawing on Midrash Tanchuma, explains that he expounded it in seventy languages, and the theme of Torah rendered in seventy tongues recurs in the Talmud’s discussion of the inscribed stones at Sotah 32a and 35b–36a. On this reading the book is not only speech but translation and explication—words multiplied so that they can be received.

    Homiletic readings sometimes note that Deuteronomy contains both consolation and rebuke—passages of love of God and severe admonition—so that the “bee” metaphor maps onto the book’s own tonal range: sweetness and sting within one swarm of words.

    Framing verses

    The book opens with devarim as its second word. The verse is unusually dense in the root ד־ב־ר, which appears three times in different guises: the words themselves (ha-devarim), the act of speaking (diber), and the place of speaking (ba-midbar, “in the wilderness”).

    אֵ֣לֶּה הַדְּבָרִ֗ים אֲשֶׁ֨ר דִּבֶּ֤ר מֹשֶׁה֙ אֶל־כׇּל־יִשְׂרָאֵ֔ל בְּעֵ֖בֶר הַיַּרְדֵּ֑ן בַּמִּדְבָּ֡ר בָּֽעֲרָבָה֩ מ֨וֹל ס֜וּף בֵּֽין־פָּארָ֧ן וּבֵֽין־תֹּ֛פֶל וְלָבָ֥ן וַחֲצֵרֹ֖ת וְדִ֥י זָהָֽב׃ Eleh ha-devarim asher diber Mosheh el kol Yisrael be-ever ha-Yarden ba-midbar, ba-Aravah mol Suf, bein Paran u-vein Tofel ve-Lavan va-Chatzerot ve-Di Zahav. “These are the words that Moses spoke to all Israel beyond the Jordan, in the wilderness, in the Arabah opposite Suph, between Paran and Tophel, Laban, Hazeroth and Di-zahab.” Deuteronomy 1:1 (emphasis added)

    The book’s closing verses do not return to bees; they return to Moses and to the audience named in the first verse. Where 1:1 reports words spoken “to all Israel” (el kol Yisrael), 34:12 ends with deeds performed “before the eyes of all Israel” (le-einei kol Yisrael), and the preceding verse insists that no prophet like Moses arose again. The frame of the book as a whole is therefore Israel as witness, with speech at the opening and sight at the close.[21]

    וְלֹא־קָ֨ם נָבִ֥יא ע֛וֹד בְּיִשְׂרָאֵ֖ל כְּמֹשֶׁ֑ה אֲשֶׁר֙ יְדָע֣וֹ יְהֹוָ֔ה פָּנִ֖ים אֶל־פָּנִֽים׃ … וּלְכֹל֙ הַיָּ֣ד הַחֲזָקָ֔ה וּלְכֹ֖ל הַמּוֹרָ֣א הַגָּד֑וֹל אֲשֶׁר֙ עָשָׂ֣ה מֹשֶׁ֔ה לְעֵינֵ֖י כׇּל־יִשְׂרָאֵֽל׃ Ve-lo kam navi od be-Yisrael ke-Mosheh, asher yeda‘o Adonai panim el panim … u-le-khol ha-yad ha-chazakah u-le-khol ha-mora ha-gadol asher asah Mosheh le-einei kol Yisrael. “Never again did there arise in Israel a prophet like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face … and for all the mighty hand and awesome power that Moses displayed before the eyes of all Israel.” Deuteronomy 34:10, 12 (emphasis added)

    The bee verse belongs to a tighter frame inside the first chapter rather than to the end of the book, as described in the following section.

    Bees within Devarim (Deuteronomy 1:44)

    The pun has a textual anchor inside the book itself. Recounting the rout of Israel after the affair of the spies, Deuteronomy 1:44 reports that the Amorites “pursued you as bees do”—the only occurrence of devorim in the Pentateuch, standing in the book called Devarim. The verse falls three verses from the end of chapter 1, so that the chapter is bracketed by the devarim of its opening words and the devorim of its closing defeat.[15]

    וַיֵּצֵ֨א הָאֱמֹרִ֜י הַיֹּשֵׁ֨ב בָּהָ֤ר הַהוּא֙ לִקְרַאתְכֶ֔ם וַיִּרְדְּפ֣וּ אֶתְכֶ֔ם כַּאֲשֶׁ֥ר תַּעֲשֶׂ֖ינָה הַדְּבֹרִ֑ים וַֽיַּכְּת֥וּ אֶתְכֶ֛ם בְּשֵׂעִ֖יר עַד־חׇרְמָֽה׃ Va-yetzei ha-Emori ha-yoshev ba-har ha-hu likratchem, va-yirdefu etchem ka-asher ta’aseinah ha-devorim, va-yaketu etchem be-Se‘ir ad Chormah. “Then the Amorites who dwelt in that hill country came out against you and pursued you as bees do, and crushed you at Seir as far as Hormah.” Deuteronomy 1:44 (emphasis added)

    In the Masoretic text the bees of 1:44 are written defectively, without a vav: הַדְּבֹרִים. Consonantally this is identical to הַדְּבָרִים, “the words,” in 1:1, and the two are distinguished only by vocalization. In an unpointed Torah scroll, where vowels are not written, the opening words of the book and the swarm that routed Israel are the same sequence of letters—a coincidence of orthography that gives the traditional wordplay something firmer than assonance.[22]

    Rashi, commenting on the verse, presses the biology of the simile: a bee dies once it has stung, and so the pursuers who struck Israel died immediately upon contact. The image therefore carries cost as well as violence—an attack that consumes the attacker. Elsewhere in Scripture the same figure appears for encircling enemies, “they surrounded me like bees” (Psalm 118:12), and for a summoned instrument of judgment, when God whistles “for the bee that is in the land of Assyria” (Isaiah 7:18).

    The counterpart to the sting is Samson’s riddle, where a swarm nests in the carcass of a lion and yields honey, prompting the formula “out of the strong came something sweet” (Judges 14:8–18). Read together, these passages give the bee a settled double valence in biblical imagery—assault and sweetness—which is the same pairing that the devarim/devorim homily applies to the words of Moses.

    Christian commentary of the modern period read the simile without reference to the Hebrew pun, and with a different emphasis. Adam Clarke’s Commentary (1831) disposes of the verse in four words—“as bees do—by irresistible numbers”—locating its force in the multitude of the pursuers rather than in the sting, the reverse of Rashi’s reading. Clarke’s fuller account of the biblical bee appears at Isaiah 5:26, where, glossing God’s “hissing” for distant nations, he adopts an explanation he traces to Cyril and Theodoret: “The metaphor is taken from the practice of those that keep bees, who draw them out of their hives into the fields, and lead them back again … by a hiss or a whistle.” He cross-references Deuteronomy 1:44 and Psalm 118:12 at that point, treating the insect consistently as a summoned army. At Psalm 118:12 he sets beside the verse the swarming-bee simile of the second book of the Iliad, quoted in Pope’s translation, and concedes the sting only in his closing analysis of the psalm, where the besiegers are “angry, and armed with stings.” At Judges 14:8 he accounts for the swarm in the lion’s carcass by the wasting away of the flesh, notes ancient reports of carcasses becoming “a receptacle for wild bees,” and quotes the Aristaeus episode of Virgil’s fourth Georgic, in which bees are generated from a dead ox. The contrast with the rabbinic material is instructive: Clarke’s interests are classical parallels and Near Eastern natural history, and the resemblance between ha-devarim and ha-devorim goes unremarked.[31]

    Linguistic richness compared with earlier books

    Among the Five Books of Moses, Deuteronomy is often singled out for the rhetorical density of its Hebrew. Even when it revisits events or commandments already narrated in Exodus, Leviticus, or Numbers, it rarely repeats them verbatim. Instead it reframes them in Moses’ voice: more hortatory, more argumentative, and more attentive to motive, memory, and consequence. The Decalogue, the appointment of judges, the episode of the spies, sabbath and festival law, and rules of war and justice all reappear with altered wording, added explanations, or shifted emphasis, so that “the same” material becomes a fresh act of teaching.[5]

    The best-known case is the Decalogue, where Exodus 20:8 commands “remember” (zakhor) the sabbath day and Deuteronomy 5:12 commands “keep” (shamor) it. Rabbinic tradition harmonizes the divergence by asserting that “zakhor and shamor were uttered in a single utterance,” a formulation found at Rosh Hashanah 27a and Shevuot 20b and applied halakhically at Berakhot 20b; the Mekhilta and the medieval commentators on both passages develop the point at length. Deuteronomy likewise supplies a different rationale for the sabbath—the memory of slavery in Egypt (Deuteronomy 5:15) rather than creation (Exodus 20:11)—so that a single commandment is transmitted with two motive clauses.[16]

    The Talmud itself registers the stylistic difference between the books. At Megillah 31b the curses of Leviticus are characterized as plural in address, and those of Deuteronomy as singular—a grammatical observation used to explain their differing liturgical placement, and one that matches the modern description of Deuteronomy as direct second-person address. Narrative retellings show the same reshaping: Rashi on Deuteronomy 1:22, comparing the request to send spies with Numbers 13, notes that the people approached Moses in disorder, unlike the orderly approach of the elders described at Deuteronomy 5:20–23.

    Medieval and modern commentators drew methodological conclusions from such variation. Abraham ibn Ezra, in his long commentary to Exodus 20:1, formulates the principle that “words are like bodies and meanings are like souls,” arguing that Scripture may preserve a sense while altering wording—an argument he uses precisely to account for the two Decalogues. Nachmanides’ introduction to Deuteronomy explains the book as commandments restated with admonition for those about to enter the land; Abarbanel treats it as oration; and later exegetes such as Malbim and the Netziv (Ha’amek Davar) build their readings on the assumption that no change of phrasing is idle. The Talmud’s image at Sanhedrin 34a—that a verse, like a hammer striking rock, scatters many sparks (Jeremiah 23:29)—supplies the underlying premise that one text yields many meanings.[17]

    Linguistically, the book favors long periodic sentences, repeated key phrases (shema, “hear”; shamor, “keep”; love of God “with all your heart”), and cascading subordinate clauses that bind law to history and history to covenant. Vocabulary of the heart, land, inheritance, and exclusive loyalty to God is especially thick. Where earlier books may present a statute as command alone, Deuteronomy characteristically surrounds it with reasons—because God redeemed Israel, because the land is a gift, because justice protects the vulnerable—producing a prose style closer to sermon and treaty than to simple legal code. That verbal surplus is part of why traditional readers hear devarim not merely as “words,” but as words that work like bees: gathering, transforming, and delivering sweetness with force.

    The syntactic contrast with the earlier books is equally marked. Deuteronomy builds long conditional periods, in which an extended protasis (“when you come into the land … and you say …”) waits several clauses for its apodosis, and it strings together purpose and motive clauses introduced by ki (“for, because”), lema’an (“in order that”) and pen (“lest”). It poses rhetorical questions of a kind almost absent from Leviticus and Numbers—“For what great nation is there that has a god so near to it …?” (4:7–8), “Has any people ever heard the voice of a god speaking out of the fire …?” (4:32–34)—and it answers them with appeals to memory. A further peculiarity, discussed in scholarship since the nineteenth century under the German term Numeruswechsel, is its abrupt alternation between singular and plural second-person address, sometimes within a single passage, which has been read variously as evidence of composite authorship or as a rhetorical device drawing the individual out of the crowd.

    Vocabulary differs in kind rather than simply in difficulty. Leviticus is dense with technical cultic terminology (classes of offering, grades of impurity, priestly procedure) and Numbers with administrative and itinerary language (censuses, tribal lists, encampments), whereas Deuteronomy’s characteristic lexicon is one of interiority and loyalty—heart, soul, love, fear, cling, remember, forget, choose—paired with a treaty vocabulary of oath, blessing and curse whose closest external analogues are Neo-Assyrian vassal treaties. Moshe Weinfeld devoted an extended appendix to cataloguing this distinctive phraseology. The poem of chapter 32 goes further still, containing a high concentration of rare words and hapax legomena that make it among the most lexically difficult passages in the Torah, and a standing challenge to translators and commentators alike.[28]

    Phonetic texture and sound-play

    Readers commonly notice that Deuteronomy is harder to read aloud than the narrative portions of Genesis or Exodus. Its Hebrew favours repetition of roots, clustered sibilants and gutturals, and rhyming verb endings, so that individual verses can behave like tongue-twisters. The first verse already stacks three occurrences of ד־ב־ר—ha-devarim, diber, ba-midbar—before closing on a jingle of place-names (bein Paran u-vein Tofel ve-Lavan va-Chatzerot ve-Di Zahav) whose labials and lameds function almost as a chant. The bee simile of 1:44, ka-asher ta’aseinah ha-devorim, packs three sibilants and two gutturals into four words and resists fluent recitation. Scholars generally describe such effects as rhetorical devices suited to oral delivery and memorisation rather than as riddles for their own sake, though the practical result for a reader is much the same.[29]

    The density is greatest in the poetry. The opening of Moses’ song summons heaven and earth with a verb from the root of speech itself (va-adaberah, “that I may speak”), then unfolds four similes bound by the repeated preposition ke-:

    הַאֲזִ֥ינוּ הַשָּׁמַ֖יִם וַאֲדַבֵּ֑רָה וְתִשְׁמַ֥ע הָאָ֖רֶץ אִמְרֵי־פִֽי׃ יַעֲרֹ֤ף כַּמָּטָר֙ לִקְחִ֔י תִּזַּ֥ל כַּטַּ֖ל אִמְרָתִ֑י כִּשְׂעִירִ֣ם עֲלֵי־דֶ֔שֶׁא וְכִרְבִיבִ֖ים עֲלֵי־עֵֽשֶׂב׃ Ha’azinu ha-shamayim va-adaberah, ve-tishma ha-aretz imrei fi. Ya‘arof ka-matar likchi, tizal ka-tal imrati, ki-se‘irim alei deshe, ve-khirvivim alei esev. “Give ear, O heavens, and let me speak; let the earth hear the words of my mouth. May my teaching drop like rain, my speech distil like dew, like showers on new grass, like droplets on the herb.” Deuteronomy 32:1–2 (emphasis added)

    Comparable effects recur throughout. Deuteronomy 32:15 rhymes three second-person verbs in sequence—shamanta avita kasita (“you grew fat, you grew thick, you were gorged”)—after a play on the name Yeshurun and the verb va-yishman. The curse of 28:37 produces an alliterative triplet, le-shamah le-mashal ve-li-shninah (“a horror, a proverb and a byword”). Shorter phrases have become proverbial precisely because of their sound: tzedek tzedek tirdof, “justice, justice shall you pursue” (16:20); Arami oved avi, “my father was a wandering Aramean” (26:5), three words alliterating on aleph; and the wilderness of 8:15 with its nachash saraf ve-akrav ve-tzimma’on, “serpent, viper, scorpion and thirst.”

    Sound and script interact in the scribal tradition as well. In the Torah scroll the ayin of shema and the dalet of echad in Deuteronomy 6:4 are written oversized, and the two enlarged letters spell ed (עד), “witness”—a visual pun embedded in the most recited verse of the book. Within Deuteronomy the effects are unevenly distributed: they cluster in the hortatory frame of chapters 1–11, in the curses of chapter 28, and in the poems of chapters 32–33, while the legal core of chapters 12–26 is comparatively plain, relying on repeated formulae rather than on assonance. Public recitation with cantillation, which segments and stresses the clauses, is the setting in which these patterns were designed to be heard.

    Related names and imagery

    The personal name Deborah (Dvora, דבורה), borne by the prophetess and judge in the Book of Judges, literally means “bee.” In Jewish interpretive tradition her name is sometimes linked to the same dual quality of speech: prophetic utterance that is sweet to the faithful and piercing to injustice. [4]

    In the biblical narrative Deborah is a premonarch leader of Israel: she holds court beneath a palm in the hill country of Ephraim, delivers God’s word as a prophetess, and summons the general Barak to fight Sisera of Hazor when tribal Israel still has no king. Judges 4–5 presents her as both juridical and military-political authority in the era of the judges—an age of charismatic, local leadership before the monarchy of Saul and David. The Song of Deborah (Judges 5), widely regarded as one of the oldest poems in the Hebrew Bible, celebrates her role and the coalition of tribes that answered her call, while also rebuking those who stayed away. Her leadership thus embodies the “bee” name in social form: speech that organizes a people, stings the oppressor, and yields a victory remembered as honeyed praise.

    Rabbinic sources treat her prominence in several registers. The Bavli at Megillah 14a counts her among seven prophetesses of Israel—Sarah, Miriam, Deborah, Hannah, Abigail, Huldah and Esther—while Pesachim 66b reads her song critically, taking the shift from “until I, Deborah, arose” (Judges 5:7) to the imperative “awake, awake, Deborah” (Judges 5:12) as evidence that prophecy withdraws from one who becomes self-important. Aggadic collections on Judges connect her name directly to the insect, explaining that a bee brings honey to its owner and stings the stranger; the tradition circulates in medieval anthologies, though its wording and placement vary between printings.[18]

    Her judicial role generated sustained halakhic discussion, since Maimonides rules that public appointments are restricted to men (Mishneh Torah, Kings 1:5). Tosafot and later authorities address the case of a woman who plainly “judged Israel,” proposing variously that Deborah taught the law rather than adjudicating, that the people accepted her authority voluntarily, or that prophecy constitutes its own warrant; Radak and Ralbag on Judges 4:4–5 emphasize her prophetic standing alongside her arbitration beneath the palm. That palm supplies the title of Moses Cordovero’s sixteenth-century ethical work Tomer Devorah (“The Palm Tree of Deborah”), in which the bee’s namesake becomes a model of imitating divine attributes.[19]

    Broader bee and honey imagery in Jewish sources—comparing Torah study to tasting honey, or the righteous to bees that produce sweetness without devouring their host—reinforces the Devarim–dvorim association even when the exact pun is not stated.

    Kabbalistic readings

    Kabbalistic literature intensifies the claim that devarim are not merely linguistic tokens but vessels of divine presence. In the classic map of the ten sefirot, speech (dibbur) is commonly assigned to Malkhut (“kingdom”), the lowest and most outward sefirah, identified with the Shekhinah, the divine presence that enters the world through language. Thought and will remain inward; speech is their disclosure. On this scheme, Moses’ farewell discourses are the archetype of revelation as utterance: what was concealed in higher worlds is articulated as words that Israel can hear.[23]

    The Zohar elaborates the point in terms of Moses’ own mouth. Passages cited throughout later kabbalistic and Hasidic writing describe the Shekhinah as speaking through Moses’ throat, so that the devarim of Deuteronomy are divine speech wearing a human voice—a reading that answers, in mystical rather than historical terms, the Talmud’s observation that Moses spoke the Deuteronomic curses “of his own accord.” Related Zoharic and midrashic–kabbalistic discussions treat the letters of the Torah as living forms: each letter is a channel of light, and the rearrangement or vocalization of letters can disclose nested meanings. The defective spelling of ha-devorim at Deuteronomy 1:44, identical in consonants to ha-devarim at 1:1, fits this sensibility readily: what looks like a bee-swarm and what looks like “words” share one written body, awaiting the pointing that distinguishes them. [24]

    A related cluster of teachings turns on the wilderness. Sefer Yetzirah and later sources treat the twenty-two Hebrew letters as instruments of creation; Hasidic masters, drawing on that inheritance and on Hosea 2:16 (“I will lead her into the wilderness and speak to her heart”), gloss midbar (“wilderness”) as the place of medabber / dibbur (“speaker / speech”). The opening of Deuteronomy, which situates Moses’ words ba-midbar, is thus read as a deliberate coincidence: the book of speech begins in the land of speech, where emptiness allows divine address to be heard. The Baal Shem Tov and his disciples extend the idea ethically: one must make of oneself a wilderness—emptied of ego—so that the words of Torah may dwell within.[25]

    Chabad Hasidism gives the letter-mysticism a systematic form. In Tanya (esp. Sha’ar ha-Yichud veha-Emunah), Shneur Zalman of Liadi teaches that the letters by which God continually “speaks” the world into being sustain every creature; were the divine utterance withdrawn for an instant, created things would revert to nothingness. Torah study, on this view, reunites the student with the generative devarim of creation. The honey–sting polarity of the Devarim–dvorim pun finds a parallel in kabbalistic ethics: words of Torah may draw down chesed (lovingkindness) or, when mishandled, expose gevurah (severity), sweetness and severity as two faces of one speech.

    Moses Cordovero’s Tomer Devorah, already noted for its title drawn from Deborah’s palm, supplies the practical counterpart. Cordovero urges the reader to imitate the thirteen attributes of mercy, beginning with forbearance toward those who offend—an ethic of speech in which rebuke, when needed, is to be as measured as a bee’s sting and as intended for the hearer’s good as honey. Later Lurianic and Hasidic writers fold such ethics into the larger drama of tikkun (repair): careful devarim gather scattered sparks; careless speech scatters them further. In that register the book of Deuteronomy is not only Moses’ last oration but a manual for how divine speech, once received, is to be spoken again by Israel.[26]

    Modern parallel: the English “spelling bee”

    English arrives at its own pairing of words and bees in the spelling bee, and the route is instructive by contrast. The relevant sense of bee denotes a gathering of neighbours for shared work or friendly competition, a usage recorded in America from 1769 and familiar from the husking bee, the quilting bee, the spinning bee and the raising bee. Dictionaries derive it not from the insect but, most probably, from dialectal been or bean—“voluntary help given to a farmer by his neighbours,” as Joseph Wright’s English Dialect Dictionary defines the related boon—going back to Middle English bene, “prayer, boon, extra service owed by a tenant to his lord.”

    The insect, on this account, entered the phrase by reanalysis. Because bees are proverbially industrious and communal, speakers heard the familiar creature in an unfamiliar dialect word and respelled it accordingly—a textbook folk etymology. The competition itself was first called a “spelling match,” attested in 1808, with “spelling bee” appearing in print by the middle of the century and spreading rapidly during a vogue for public contests in the 1870s. Noah Webster’s American Spelling Book of 1783, the “Blue-backed Speller,” supplied the material, and a national contest has been held in the United States since 1925.[30]

    The comparison with devarim and devorim is therefore a comparison of two mechanisms rather than of two etymologies. Both phrases join speech to bees on the strength of sound alone. But the English idiom does so unknowingly, having lost the dialect word it replaced, whereas midrash performs the same operation deliberately and openly, asserting a homiletical connection while leaving the plain sense of the text intact. Folk etymology mistakes resemblance for descent; derash uses resemblance without making that claim.

    Scholarly notes

    Academic treatments distinguish folk etymology and midrashic paronomasia from historical derivation. The educational force of the pun does not require that davar and dvora share a common root; it requires only that Hebrew speakers hear a usable resemblance. Similar sound-play is widespread in biblical poetry and rabbinic literature, where assonance and consonant reuse generate theological meaning. [3]

    Wordplay in the Hebrew Bible has been catalogued since Immanuel Casanowicz’s Paronomasia in the Old Testament (1894), and Moshe Garsiel’s Biblical Names (1991) analyses the specific phenomenon of names explained through sound rather than derivation—the category to which Devorah belongs. Studies of rabbinic method, from Isaak Heinemann’s Darkhei ha-Aggadah to Daniel Boyarin’s Intertextuality and the Reading of Midrash, argue that such readings are best understood as a hermeneutic of association rather than failed philology. On Deuteronomy’s language, the standard treatments are Moshe Weinfeld’s Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School and Jeffrey Tigay’s commentary, both of which document the book’s formulaic phraseology and its reuse of earlier material.[20]
