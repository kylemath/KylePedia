# Editorial review: “Devarim–dvorim wordplay”

This review covers the article as it stood in the baseline commit `3f709ce`.
During revision, the entry was moved from `index.html` to
`articles/devarim-dvorim-wordplay.html`; the review follows the original section
order so that each decision can be compared with that commit.

## Overall assessment

The original article had three substantial strengths: it placed Hebrew beside
translation, assembled interpretations from several periods, and noticed the
important consonantal identity between Deuteronomy 1:1 and 1:44. Its principal
weakness was accretion. Each new topic had been appended without revising the
argument around it, producing repetition, blurred distinctions between sources
and the article’s own proposals, and several conclusions stated more strongly
than the evidence allowed.

The revision follows four editorial rules:

1. Identify the direct source first. The central honey-and-sting interpretation
   is not merely a teaching that “circulates”; it is in *Devarim Rabbah* 1:6
   (1:5 in some editions).
2. Separate philology, *peshat*, midrash, later reception, and modern synthesis.
   A useful homily need not be a historical etymology or an authorial pun.
3. Compare genres rather than rank them. Deuteronomy has distinctive rhetoric,
   but “richer,” “harder,” and “more complex” require defined criteria.
4. Attribute interpretations and state limits. Phrases such as “precisely the
   moral,” “the archetype,” and “a manual for” often converted an analogy into a
   conclusion without a cited source.

## Paragraph-level review

### Lead

1. **“The Devarim–dvorim wordplay is a Hebrew linguistic and midrashic
   association…” — revise.** The paragraph defined the subject clearly but
   called it “linguistic” before distinguishing wordplay from etymology and cited
   only a vague circulating tradition. The revision names *Devarim Rabbah* and
   states immediately that the association is midrashic, not a demonstrated
   cognate relationship.

2. **“Modern English offers a coincidental parallel…” — shorten and defer.**
   This detailed English excursus interrupted the Hebrew introduction and was
   repeated later. The revision gives it one sentence in the lead and retains the
   evidence in a dedicated reception section.

### Linguistic background

3. **“In Hebrew, davar means ‘word,’ ‘thing,’ or ‘matter’…” — keep with
   precision.** The basic definition was sound. The revision adds *diber* and
   *dibbur* and avoids implying that the title is an independent linguistic
   argument.

4. **“In Modern Hebrew, speakers usually distinguish…” — revise.** The
   *milah/devar* contrast was useful but too categorical and ended with the
   rhetorical phrase “not a pile of dictionary words, but weighty spoken
   matters.” The revision describes overlapping usage and lets context determine
   the preferred noun.

5. **“The distinction is a late redistribution…” — revise.** The biblical and
   Aramaic evidence was valuable, but the conclusion that the book “could only”
   be titled *Devarim* overstated what the evidence establishes. The revision
   explains incipit naming and historical usage without a counterfactual.

6. **“Even so, the verbal root of speech remains…” — split and reduce.** This
   paragraph combined Modern Hebrew morphology, Moses’ speech impediment,
   *Devarim Rabbah*, and an unrelated homograph involving circumcision. The
   revision keeps morphology in the linguistic section and moves Moses’ healed
   speech to classical interpretation. The circumcision homograph is removed
   because it was peripheral and unsourced.

7. **“By contrast, dvora means ‘bee’…” — revise.** The paragraph correctly
   separated the lexical items but called them “near-homophones” without noting
   their different vowels. The revision uses “paronomasia,” states the phonetic
   difference, and cites lexicons for the disputed etymology.

8. **“Rabbinic literature supplies an explicit warrant…” — keep, narrow.**
   *Al tikrei* and *notarikon* give useful methodological context, but they do not
   prove that every sound resemblance was intended by the biblical text. The
   revision presents them as tools of rabbinic exposition.

9. **“Lexicographers differ on whether devorah belongs to the root…” —
   revise.** The paragraph joined an etymological dispute to a *midbar* homily and
   implied that “ordered speech” and an “ordered swarm” might be cognates. The
   revision distinguishes the pasture/drive derivation of *midbar* from later
   speech homilies and avoids treating order as evidence.

### Midrashic interpretation

10. **“Rabbinic and later Jewish teachers frequently liken Torah…” — replace
    with the primary source.** The claim was true but unnecessarily vague. The
    revision sets out *Devarim Rabbah*’s separate bee interpretations and quotes
    the honey-and-sting clause directly, so that the article’s central evidence is
    visible rather than summarized.

11. **“The double effect has a classical Talmudic formulation…” — qualify.**
    *Yoma* 72b and *Taanit* 7a are parallels, not sources for the bee reading.
    “Precisely the moral” made an unsupported identification. The revision states
    that neither passage mentions bees, and distinguishes the three groups the
    elixir-and-potion image is applied to: those who keep or transgress the
    commandments, Israel and idolaters, and the worthy or unworthy student.

12. **“The sting also has a scriptural cognate in the goad…” — keep, temper.**
    Ecclesiastes and *Chagigah* give a useful comparison, but “give Torah’s words
    a mouth and a point” was ornamental rather than analytical. The revision
    reports the image directly.

13. **“The honey side of the comparison is developed most fully…” — keep,
    separate.** The sources were relevant but represented different genres and
    periods. The revision distinguishes biblical honey, aggadah, *Sifrei*, and the
    medieval initiation rite and notes that the rite was not based specifically
    on Deuteronomy’s title. *Sifrei* 306 is described as collecting several
    interpretations of the rain and dew figures, not one graded scale of
    gentleness and force.

14. **“Halakhic literature contributes a further twist…” — qualify.**
    *Bekhorot* 7b concerns the legal status of honey. The sentence “sweetness may
    pass intact through a stinging vessel” was the article’s homily, not the
    Talmud’s. The revision labels any such connection as later sermonic use.

15. **“Commentators also develop secondary images…” — remove.** The hive,
    collective-work, and durability analogies were not attributed to identifiable
    sources and repeated the article’s conclusion in increasingly decorative
    language.

### Deuteronomy as speech

16. **“Deuteronomy presents itself as a book of speech…” — keep, neutralize.**
    The observation is central, but “living, purposeful speech rather than inert
    text” was a false contrast. The revision describes speech, writing, hearing,
    and recitation as features that coexist in the book.

17. **“Midrash Halakhah defines the genre…” — keep.** *Sifrei* and Rashi on
    rebuke belong here. The revision removes the final assertion that this
    “makes the bee comparison apt,” which was interpretive commentary rather than
    a source claim.

18. **“Talmudic discussion of the book’s voice reinforces the point…” —
    revise and split conceptually.** The paragraph compressed *Megillah*,
    Nachmanides, Kabbalah, Talmudic authorship, and Adam Clarke. The revision keeps
    Jewish models of Moses’ voice together and treats Clarke under later
    reception.

19. **“Deuteronomy 1:5 says that Moses began to expound…” — keep.** The
    seventy-languages tradition is relevant to the book’s self-presentation as
    explanation. The revision avoids “words multiplied so that they can be
    received,” which restated the point rhetorically.

20. **“Homiletic readings sometimes note…” — remove.** “Sweetness and sting
    within one swarm of words” repeated the thesis without adding a source or a
    new distinction.

### Framing verses

21. **“The book opens with devarim as its second word…” — keep with an
    etymological caution.** *Ha-devarim* and *diber* are related; *midbar* repeats
    the consonants but its connection to speech is homiletical. The revision no
    longer calls all three occurrences of one root.

22. **“The book’s closing verses do not return to bees…” — keep and qualify.**
    The “all Israel” correspondence is a plausible *inclusio*, but “therefore
    Israel as witness” was stated as settled fact. The revision attributes it to a
    literary reading.

23. **“The bee verse belongs to a tighter frame…” — revise.** The sentence
    appropriately corrected the earlier bookending premise, but called the
    chapter frame definite. The revision labels it a proposal and notes that
    chapter divisions are later.

### Bees in Deuteronomy 1:44

24. **“The pun has a textual anchor…” — keep, qualify.** The verse is the only
    Pentateuchal occurrence of plural “bees,” but “the chapter is bracketed” was
    stronger than the evidence. The revision offers a possible local echo and
    preserves the ordinary military sense.

25. **“In the Masoretic text the bees…are written defectively…” — keep,
    clarify.** This is the strongest close-reading observation in the article.
    The revision explains that “defective” is a technical spelling term, that the
    vowel points distinguish the nouns, and that the identity does not prove
    shared origin or intention.

26. **“Rashi…presses the biology…” — revise and broaden.** Rashi’s reading is
    reported accurately, but the original treated a worker honeybee’s death after
    stinging as universal. The revision places Rashi beside Ibn Ezra and Chizkuni
    and reserves biological qualification for the critical section.

27. **“The counterpart to the sting is Samson’s riddle…” — keep, temper.**
    Judges supplies an important sweetness image, but “settled double valence”
    made a small set of texts sound exhaustive. The revision calls them biblical
    precedents used by later interpretation.

28. **“Christian commentary of the modern period…” — condense.** The Adam
    Clarke material was accurate and interesting but disproportionately long. The
    revision retains his “irresistible numbers,” classical comparisons, and
    silence about the Hebrew pun in a compact reception paragraph.

### Retelling and rhetorical style

29. **“Among the Five Books of Moses, Deuteronomy is often singled out…” —
    revise.** “Rhetorical density” and “fresh act of teaching” were evaluative.
    The revision identifies concrete changes and sets traditional harmonization
    beside source-critical analysis.

30. **“The best-known case is the Decalogue…” — keep, balance.** The
    *zakhor/shamor* tradition and differing sabbath rationales are central. The
    revision adds the alternative that the versions may reflect different legal
    formulations.

31. **“The Talmud itself registers the stylistic difference…” — replace.**
    This paragraph mixed the curses, liturgy, and spies. The revision gives the
    spies’ accounts their own comparison and distinguishes harmonizing and
    source-critical readings.

32. **“Medieval and modern commentators drew methodological conclusions…” —
    keep, reduce.** Ibn Ezra’s body/soul analogy is valuable. The hammer-and-sparks
    ending was a general hermeneutic flourish rather than evidence for the specific
    differences under discussion, so it is omitted.

33. **“Linguistically, the book favors long periodic sentences…” — revise.**
    The descriptive center was strong, but “verbal surplus,” “words that work like
    bees,” and the sermon-versus-“simple legal code” contrast were evaluative. The
    revision identifies recurring vocabulary and compares genre-specific lexicons.

34. **“The syntactic contrast…is equally marked…” — keep, qualify.**
    Conditional syntax, motive clauses, rhetorical questions, and
    *Numeruswechsel* belong in the analysis. “Almost absent” required a corpus
    count; the revision removes it and lists competing explanations.

35. **“Vocabulary differs in kind rather than simply in difficulty…” — keep,
    refine.** This was the section’s best controlling insight. The revision
    retains genre-specific vocabulary, qualifies the treaty comparison, and
    prevents the difficult poetry of chapter 32 from standing for the entire book.

### Sound patterning

36. **“Readers commonly notice that Deuteronomy is harder to read aloud…” —
    revise substantially.** No reader study or quantitative comparison supported
    the claim. “Jingle,” “almost as a chant,” and “resists fluent recitation”
    converted subjective experience into fact. The revision names consonant
    clusters and allows that learners may experience them as tongue-twister-like.

37. **“The density is greatest in the poetry…” — keep with scope.** Chapters
    32–33 are formally poetic and justify closer sound analysis. The quotation is
    retained.

38. **“Comparable effects recur throughout…” — keep, correct the
    conclusion.** The examples are useful. The claim that phrases became
    proverbial “precisely because of their sound” was causal speculation. The
    revision reports the forms and notes that similar techniques occur elsewhere.

39. **“Sound and script interact…” — keep, qualify.** The enlarged letters of
    the *Shema* are relevant scribal reception. The claim that patterns “were
    designed” for Masoretic cantillation was anachronistic; the revision treats
    cantillation as a later received performance tradition.

### Deborah

40. **“The personal name Deborah…” — keep, delimit.** Her name does mean
    “bee,” but the original made prophetic speech sweet and piercing without a
    direct source. The revision states that Judges does not connect her name to
    Deuteronomy’s title.

41. **“In the biblical narrative Deborah is a premonarch leader…” — keep,
    remove homiletic closure.** Her judicial, prophetic, and political roles are
    relevant. “Stings the oppressor” and “honeyed praise” were modern rhetoric
    presented as analysis.

42. **“Rabbinic sources treat her prominence…” — keep, source carefully.**
    *Megillah* and *Pesachim* are identifiable and are retained. The claim that
    aggadic anthologies explain Deborah’s name through the bee’s honey and sting
    could not be verified at any specific locus, so it is removed rather than
    hedged. The footnote now states that *Pesachim* 66b treats her self-reference
    as a warning against pride and does not develop a honey-and-sting reading.

43. **“Her judicial role generated sustained halakhic discussion…” — keep,
    clarify *Tomer Devorah*.** The legal debate is useful. Cordovero’s title comes
    from Judges 4:5, but his work is not about Deborah or the bee pun; the revision
    says so explicitly.

44. **“Broader bee and honey imagery…” — remove.** The paragraph generalized
    from unspecified sources and repeated the thesis.

### Kabbalistic and Hasidic reception

45. **“Kabbalistic literature intensifies the claim…” — revise.** General
    symbolism of *dibbur* and Malkhut is relevant reception history, but the
    original made it a claim about these particular *devarim*. The revision marks
    the distinction.

46. **“The Zohar elaborates the point…” — replace with an exact locus.** The
    original cited a formula “widely” attributed to the Zohar without a page and
    then applied letter mysticism to 1:44. The revision cites Zohar III 7a and
    states that it concerns Moses’ voice, not the bee pun.

47. **“A related cluster of teachings turns on the wilderness…” — keep,
    qualify attribution.** The *midbar/medabber* homily is relevant but not
    etymology. Claims about the Baal Shem Tov varied by anthology, so the revision
    avoids a precise attribution it could not document.

48. **“Chabad Hasidism gives the letter-mysticism…” — keep the source, remove
    the application.** *Tanya*, *Sha’ar ha-Yichud veha-Emunah* 1, does teach
    continuous creation through divine letters. It does not map the bee pun onto
    *chesed* and *gevurah*. The revision states that applying it to 1:1/1:44 is a
    modern extension.

49. **“Moses Cordovero’s Tomer Devorah…supplies the practical counterpart” —
    remove.** The paragraph inferred an ethic of measured stinging and spark
    gathering that was not sourced to Cordovero. Relevant information about the
    book’s title remains in the Deborah section.

### Spelling bee

50. **“English arrives at its own pairing…” — keep, shorten.** The etymology is
    a useful modern comparison but should not rival the article’s Hebrew subject.

51. **“The insect…entered the phrase by reanalysis…” — qualify.** Major
    dictionaries mark the dialect derivation as possible rather than certain. The
    revision retains the dates and educational context without claiming a settled
    route.

52. **“The comparison…is therefore a comparison of two mechanisms…” —
    revise.** The folk-etymology/midrash distinction is useful, but “unknowingly”
    and “mistakes resemblance for descent” personified a language community. The
    revision states the methodological difference without assigning intention.

### Scholarly notes

53. **“Academic treatments distinguish folk etymology…” — merge into the new
    critical section.** The point now frames the article’s limits rather than
    appearing after all interpretation is complete.

54. **“Wordplay in the Hebrew Bible has been catalogued…” — redistribute.**
    Casanowicz, Garsiel, Heinemann, Boyarin, Weinfeld, and Tigay address different
    questions. The revision cites them at the specific claims they support instead
    of grouping them as general authority.

### Reference caveat and footer prose

55. **“Citations follow standard printed editions…” — keep and strengthen.**
    The caveat is necessary, especially where anthology numbering varies. The
    revised notes now flag modern extensions directly rather than relying only on
    a blanket disclaimer.

56. **Site footer and navigation prose — keep.** These belong to the Kypedia
    interface rather than the argument. Relative asset paths were corrected after
    the article moved into `articles/`.

## Conceptual balance added

The revised **Alternative interpretations and critical perspectives** section
now presents objections that a critical reader could reasonably make:

- the words need not be cognates for the midrash to function;
- identical unpointed spelling does not demonstrate biblical authorial intent;
- Deuteronomy 1:44 is not the end of the book and may be ordinary military imagery;
- “complexity” depends on whether one measures vocabulary, syntax, genre, or oral
  performance;
- “tongue-twister” is experiential unless supported by a defined phonetic metric;
- Rashi’s bee biology describes one common stinging event, not every bee or sting;
- kabbalistic doctrines cited here do not themselves comment on Deuteronomy 1:44;
- traditional and historical-critical authorship models ask different questions;
- exclusivist or punitive formulations in inherited sources can be reported
  without being endorsed.

The goal of these changes is not to disprove the wordplay. It is to make clear
what kind of claim is being made at each stage: lexical, textual, midrashic,
literary, mystical, comparative, or devotional.

## Follow-up pass

A second pass closed the gaps between this review and the article, and applied
several points of precision that the first revision had left open.

- **Moses as speaker restored to its proper section.** Item 6 removed his
  “I am not a man of words” material from the linguistic background but did not
  place it anywhere. It now appears in the classical interpretation section with
  *Devarim Rabbah* 1:1 on the healing of his tongue and R. Tanchuma’s parable of
  the purple cloth. The unsourced *milah*/circumcision homograph remains out.
- **The source midrash is quoted.** A short block quotation of the honey-and-sting
  clause from *Devarim Rabbah* 1:6 replaces a paraphrase of the article’s own
  central evidence.
- **The exclusionary version is stated where it occurs.** The critical section
  already mentioned Torah as “an elixir of life for Israel and a potion of death
  for idolaters”; the classical section now reports it too, so the objection is
  raised about a formulation the reader has actually seen.
- **Three applications of the elixir image are distinguished** rather than merged
  into one moral about the recipient.
- **Clarke’s authorship view returns as reception.** Item 18 called for it; the
  first pass deleted it. His judgement that Deuteronomy 1:1–5 was added “probably
  either by Joshua or Ezra” now sits with his other comments and points forward to
  the discussion of compositional models.
- **Verse numbering and footnote order.** Hosea 2:16 is given with its common
  English numbering, and the reference list was renumbered in order of first
  appearance so that the note markers and the printed list agree without the
  ordering workaround the first pass had used.

## Stylistic pass

The second revision was accurate but had acquired a habit of narrating
disciplines instead of reporting findings: “a historical linguist asks whether…,
a midrash asks what…”, “traditional commentators reconcile, while
historical-critical scholarship asks…”, “source-critical readings instead
examine…”. The effect was of a writer trying on hats rather than of a reader who
has been through the material and reached a view. A third pass rewrote those
passages to state the analysis directly and to say who holds what, without
staging a comparison of methods.

Related changes:

- **The critical section was dissolved into the body.** “Alternative
  interpretations and critical perspectives” had become an appendix of bolded
  verdicts, each repeating a subject the article had already covered. Every point
  now sits with the material it judges: the limits of the etymology in the
  linguistic background, authorial intent beside the spelling of 1:44, the
  commentators’ bee biology with the commentators, the chapter-frame correction in
  the section on the book’s frames, the “complexity” argument with the comparison
  of vocabulary, audience-dependence with the sound patterning, the mystical
  material with the Kabbalah, dating with Moses’ voice, and the exclusionary
  version of the midrash beside the midrash itself.
- **`#Scholarly_notes` was kept and repurposed.** The authoring specification
  expects that section in a `stable` entry, so the id remains, now headed
  *Assessment* and carrying two paragraphs of summary judgement rather than a
  checklist. No page linked to the old heading text.
- **Repeated disclaimers were consolidated.** Four separate sentences had
  explained that a source “is not, by itself, an interpretation of the bee
  comparison.” The Kabbalah section now says this once, at the top, and then
  presents its sources.
- **Self-reference removed.** The lead no longer tells the reader what the
  article will distinguish, and the body no longer reports what “the article”
  does.
- **Footnotes re-pointed.** Moving the critical material shifted several markers
  onto the wrong notes; each citation was checked against the content of the note
  it cites, and the list renumbered again by first appearance.
