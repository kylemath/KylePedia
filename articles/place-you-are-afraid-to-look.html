    The place you are most afraid to look

    This entry is a dvar Torah (a Torah sermon) written for Yom Kippur. The short form is given first, in the words in which it is spoken; the longer version follows. It speaks directly to "you," in the second person, because that is how a sermon is delivered. For a fuller account of the superego as an obligation toward repair, and of how loss can turn that obligation cruel, see The Divine Fracture.

    Occasion Yom Kippur morning, a Reform congregation

    Morning reading Deuteronomy 29:9–14; 30:11–20
    Formula נַעֲשֶׂה וְנִשְׁמָע

    Closing גְּמַר חֲתִימָה טוֹבָה

    The place you are most afraid to look is where Yom Kippur says joy is found. Modern culture sells a shallow, upbeat mood: every feeling is treated as valid, and every option is left open, so nothing commits us and nothing costs us anything. Yom Kippur makes a different and harder claim. The lasting joy we actually want is not reached by avoiding discipline. It is found inside the discipline we have been avoiding: a boundary we keep, a practice we follow, an action we take before we feel ready to take it.

    Two losses sit in the room with that claim. A friend has died. A cousin's yahrzeit falls this year. This sermon will not solve grief; grief is not a problem to be solved. But grief is also, without our noticing it, being used to excuse a second and different fear: the fear of committing to a real practice before we feel we have earned the right to — before we have the belief, the healing, or the certainty we assume we need first. The way through this fear is old, and it can be stated in one sentence. Act first, and understanding will follow. You already have what the acting requires.

      The short version

      Spoken form of the sermon. The longer version, with its sources, follows.

      We live in a culture obsessed with “happy-happy, joy-joy”—a high-gloss world painted in an endless, anything-goes rainbow where every feeling must be curated and valid. But when real life crashes in—the raw ache of a friend’s death, the empty chair at the holiday table, the heavy silence of a yahrzeit—that technicolor veneer instantly washes out.

      Yom Kippur cuts through the glare with a stark, unsettling premise: the radical, resilient joy you seek is hidden in the exact place you are most terrified to look.

      What we truly fear isn’t just pain; it is obligation. We are drowning in the blinding rainbow of boundless choices, paralyzing subjectivity, and shifting moods. To find relief, we actually have to step out of that chaotic rainbow and back toward the black-and-white. We need the stark, clear boundaries of sacred commitment.

      Yet we freeze, assuming we must leap straight from the blinding glare into pure certainty. But here is what they don’t tell you: when you step out of that rainbow room of waiting, you do not instantly arrive in clarity or perfect peace. To get to genuine color, you have to walk through the shades of grey.

      Those shades of grey are our daily actions. How do we navigate them?

      John Wesley, the founder of Methodism, once wrestled with his own paralyzing doubt. His mentor gave him radical advice: “Preach faith until you have it; and then, because you have it, you will preach faith.” Judaism has echoed this for millennia: Na’aseh v’nishma—we will do, and then we will understand. In other words: let your feet lead your heart.

      You do not wait for pure, vibrant belief to act. You walk into the places you fear by stepping directly into the grey area of practice. When grief paralyzes you, the path to relief isn’t found in waiting; it is found by aligning your thinking, feeling, and acting today.

      Don’t wait for emotional closure to sit shiva with a neighbor or bring the meal. Don’t wait to feel pious or certain to light the candles. Show up for the minyan. Say the prayer. The concrete deed is not a hypocritical performance of faith you lack; it is the exact engine that generates it. The black-and-white frame of tradition is the scaffolding that holds you safe while you work through the messy, ambiguous shades of grey.

      We hesitate because we buy into the illusion of the rainbow—the myth that we must be entirely whole and inspired before we can even begin. The truth is, you are standing here today with absolutely everything you need to do exactly what you were meant to do.

      Stop chasing the shallow “happy-happy” veneer. Step back toward the black-and-white discipline of this holy day, do the honest work in the grey, and claim the fierce, authentic joy waiting on the other side.

      G’mar Chatimah Tovah.

      Happy-happy, joy-joy

      Much of ordinary life is spent keeping up a pleasant surface. That feeling is real, but it is brief and it costs us nothing, because it works by keeping hard things at a distance: grief that will not resolve on a schedule, obligations that do not wait until we feel like meeting them, beliefs that would require more than a passing interest. Call this surface happy-happy, joy-joy. As a mood, it is genuine. As a way to live, it accomplishes nothing. It asks nothing of us and commits us to nothing, and it survives only because we never look closely at anything for very long.

      Yom Kippur does not ask us to perform that mood more convincingly. The confession, the fast, standing together in prayer, naming what we have done and failed to do — none of this is meant to produce cheerfulness. The day assumes something different: that real joy can exist in the same room as shame, inadequacy, and mourning. That joy does not arrive as an improved version of the pleasant surface. When it arrives, it arrives as relief — the relief of a person who has stopped managing how they appear and has simply done the next right thing.

      The central claim of the day can be stated plainly: what you are most desperate to find is waiting exactly where you are most afraid to look. For some of us, that place is the plain fact of a death. For many of us, if we are honest, the place is smaller and more uncomfortable than that. It is the moment we are asked to take on an obligation. It is saying what we believe out loud. It is acting before we are sure we are right to act.

      Grief, and the fear under it

      We tell ourselves a flattering explanation for why we avoid this moment. We say we are avoiding pain: the fresh grief of a friend's death, the older grief of a cousin's yahrzeit, the empty place these two facts leave in a holiday about renewal. That explanation is half true. Grief is heavy, and no single paragraph resolves it. You live through it. You mark the date. You let the absence remain an absence, rather than trying to fill it.

      The other half of the explanation is less flattering, and it is the part that matters here. Often what we fear most is not the pain itself, but the cure for it: belief, and the action that belief requires. Grief can stay private. A practice has to be done in front of other people. Grief only asks to be felt. A commitment asks to be kept even on mornings when our feelings have not caught up with it. If we look directly at how small we feel, how unfinished our mourning is, how thin our faith is, we assume we will fall apart. So we wait — for the grief to pass on its own, or for a sudden certainty to arrive — and we call that waiting patience or honesty, when it is really avoidance.

      What comes after the waiting, if we stop waiting, follows a specific order: grief, then belief, then relief. That order is the teaching. Relief does not replace grief or cancel it out. Relief becomes possible once belief stops being a feeling you are waiting to have and becomes a practice you are already doing. You cannot think your way into belief, and you cannot think your way out of grief. Thinking is one part of what you bring to the practice. It is not a requirement you must satisfy before the practice can begin.

      The superego that withholds permission

      There is a name for the inner voice that enforces this waiting. In psychoanalysis it is called the superego: the internal standard that judges us, sets our ideals, and forbids certain actions. A fuller account of this part of the mind — how it demands repair when it works well, and how it turns cruel after a loss — is the subject of a longer entry in this library, The Divine Fracture. Freud described both of these moods. The superego says both “you ought to” and “you may not.” After a death, it can take anger that belonged to the person we lost and direct it at ourselves instead, so that mourning turns into a verdict that we are worthless.[1] What follows uses the word "superego" in that broad sense, as a way of naming a hesitation many people in this room share. It is not a diagnosis of anyone present.

      This freeze shows up in two different ways, though both are the same underlying refusal. The first way is rebellion. A modern, liberal community has spent generations learning, for good reasons, to be suspicious of anything that feels like compulsion. Fixed law, ritual, and set words can sound like someone else's superego — an authority we grew up under and are supposed to have outgrown. The historical term for rejecting binding religious law is antinomian. Reform Judaism is not that rejection: it moved religious authority from a code enforced by threat to a covenant that a community chooses to keep. But the instinct behind that history is still present in this room. We would rather keep every option open than commit to a single, fixed line of practice. Obligation feels like something to escape, not something to enter.

      The second way never rebels. It waits for permission instead. In its harshest mood, the superego insists that we have everything figured out before we are allowed to begin: intellectual certainty, a pure motive, grief that has already been processed, feelings that match the words we would say. Until all of that is true, it tells us, any practice would be hypocrisy. This is how a high standard turns into a reason never to start. We are not permitted to show up as we actually are. We are only permitted to show up once we have already become the person who would no longer need the practice — which is to say, never.

      Put these two responses together, and the feared place becomes clear. We tell ourselves we are avoiding pain, or avoiding an unfair authority. But underneath both stories is the same refusal: we will not commit to a specific belief and a specific action while we are still doubting, still grieving, still incomplete. The rebelling superego and the permission-withholding superego produce the same result. Both keep the work inside our heads instead of in our hands. Both let us call delay a form of honesty. And the cure is the very commitment that the delay exists to avoid making.

      Out of the rainbow

      A philosophical thought experiment gives us a useful image, though this sermon is going to turn it around. The philosopher Frank Jackson describes Mary, a brilliant scientist who learns every physical fact about color — the wavelengths involved, the optics, the neuroscience of how the brain processes the sight of a tomato or a sky — while spending her whole life in a room with no color in it, studying the world only through a black-and-white monitor. Jackson's question is whether Mary learns something new on the day she finally leaves the room and sees color for herself. He argues that she does, and uses this to argue that physical facts alone do not capture everything there is to know.[2] This sermon borrows only the image, not Jackson's full philosophical conclusion, which is discussed in the notes below. The image makes one point clearly: knowing facts about red is not the same as experiencing red. Faith and emotional healing work the same way. You cannot complete them by thinking about them from a safe distance.

      Here is where this congregation's situation reverses Mary's. We are not trapped in a colorless room, starved for experience. If anything, we have the opposite problem: we are surrounded by too many options, too many competing feelings, and too many claims that everything is equally true and equally valid. That is what the happy-happy, joy-joy culture actually is — unlimited choice with no fixed points to stand on. What this congregation needs is not more options. It is the experience of a boundary: a fixed practice, a set liturgy, a commitment that does not change every time our mood changes.

      Taking on that kind of commitment does not feel vivid or transformative right away. Jackson's Mary sees the full color of a rose the moment she leaves her room. We do not get that instant transformation. Between the fixed obligation and a faith that feels fully our own, there is a long stretch of daily, ordinary effort — shades of grey. That daily effort is not a compromise, and it is not a sign that we have failed to commit fully. It is simply what practice looks like before it becomes second nature: still a deliberate choice, still feeling somewhat imposed from outside, but already real and already doing its work.

      A second example makes the same point in music. Give a musician every key, every tempo, and no fixed structure, and the result is not music — it is noise. A great solo happens inside a set of constraints: a chord progression, a tempo, a structure the musician can lean on and push against. The fixed structure is the black-and-white. A solo that eventually feels like the musician's own voice is the color. The long practice of playing inside that structure, before it feels natural, is the grey area in between. Unlimited choice was never what produced joy. It produced only overwhelm.

      Do, and then understand

      The way into that grey area is exactly the thing the superego forbids: acting without being certain first. In early March 1738, John Wesley — before Methodism existed as an organized movement — spent several days in Oxford convinced that he lacked real faith. The thought that came to him was the superego's exact argument: stop preaching, because you cannot honestly preach a faith you do not have. He asked his mentor, Peter Böhler, whether he should stop. Böhler told him not to. Wesley asked what he could possibly preach instead. Böhler's answer was:

      “Preach faith till you have it; and then, because you have it, you will preach faith.” John Wesley, journal, 5 March 1738, reporting Peter Böhler[3]

      Wesley began preaching two days later, on Monday. He records in his journal that he did so “though my soul started back from the work” — meaning he felt resistance to it the whole time. He did not wait for that resistance to end before he acted. The preaching itself was the instrument that produced the faith he did not yet feel he had. This is a specifically Christian story, but its logic applies outside Christianity as well.

      Aristotle described the same order centuries earlier, as the process by which a person's character is formed. He distinguished intellectual virtue, which can be taught directly, from moral virtue, which is a habit built by repeating the actions that express it. We become builders by building, he wrote, and musicians by playing music; in the same way, “we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts.”[4] He drew a careful distinction here: our senses, like sight and hearing, are abilities we have before we ever use them — we do not develop eyesight by practicing looking. But virtue works the opposite way. We perform the action first, and the settled character trait follows from repeating it. Mary, in Jackson's thought experiment, is on the side of the senses and of factual knowledge: she can have complete information and still lack an experience. Courage, justice, and a lived faith belong to the other category, the one Aristotle describes: we acquire them only by practicing them, before we have fully become the kind of person who has them. Jackson and Aristotle were not writing about the same subject, but read together, they point this congregation toward the same conclusion.

      Judaism makes the same claim about the revelation at Sinai, and treats it as significant enough to call a secret. When the book of the covenant was read aloud to the people, they answered:

      כֹּל אֲשֶׁר־דִּבֶּר יְהוָה נַעֲשֶׂה וְנִשְׁמָע Kol asher-dibber Adonai na'aseh v'nishma. “All that the Lord has spoken we will do, and we will hear.” Exodus 24:7

      The Hebrew word nishma means “we will hear,” and in this context it also implies obeying and understanding. Rabbinic commentary focuses on the order of the two verbs: doing comes before hearing. In the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Shabbat 88a, a heavenly voice asks who revealed to Israel this secret used by the angels — a reference to Psalm 103:20, where angels are described as carrying out God's word and only afterward attending to its meaning. The same passage compares Israel to an apple tree whose fruit appears before its leaves grow, meaning the action comes before the explanation.[5] This is the same claim this sermon is making. The joy is hidden in the place you fear because that place is exactly where action has to come before understanding.

      A simpler, more familiar version of this same idea is the phrase “just do it.” Align your thinking, feeling, speaking, and acting now, using whatever you actually have available, rather than waiting for better materials. That phrase was written to sell athletic shoes, but the principle behind it is much older than the advertisement. The sources above — Wesley, Aristotle, and the Talmud — are the ones worth remembering.

      Mouth, heart, and deed

      This morning's Torah reading has already been making this argument, before the rest of this sermon caught up to it. In Reform congregations, the Yom Kippur morning reading comes from parashat Nitzavim, Deuteronomy 29:9–14 and 30:11–20, replacing the traditional reading of the Temple ritual in Leviticus 16. Later prayer books, including Mishkan HaNefesh, keep this reading and also offer the Leviticus chapter as an alternative.[6] Both readings place a person inside a strict boundary before that person feels ready for it. Leviticus 16 opens with the death of Aaron's two sons and then restricts the high priest's access to the innermost sanctuary: he may not enter whenever he wishes, and only according to a fixed ritual.[7] Nitzavim makes the same point differently, by speaking directly to the reader in the second person — which is characteristic of how Deuteronomy addresses its audience throughout.[8]

      אַתֶּם נִצָּבִים הַיּוֹם כֻּלְּכֶם לִפְנֵי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם Atem nitzavim ha-yom kulkhem lifnei Adonai Eloheikhem. “You are standing today, all of you, before the Lord your God.” Deuteronomy 29:9

      Look around this room. This verse does not require interpretation to apply to us — it describes what is happening right now. You are standing here today, all of you: the confident and the uncertain, the grieving and the simply tired, people who came because they believe and people who came because someone else asked them to. The covenant described in the verses that follow is made with everyone standing here, and also with those who are not physically present. A yahrzeit is one way that people no longer here are still included in that standing.

      The reading then rules out a specific excuse: that the instruction we need is somewhere else — in heaven, across the sea, or in a certainty we have not yet reached:

      כִּי־קָרוֹב אֵלֶיךָ הַדָּבָר מְאֹד בְּפִיךָ וּבִלְבָבְךָ לַעֲשֹׂתוֹ Ki-karov elekha ha-davar me'od, be-fikha u-vilvavekha la'asoto. “For the word is very near to you: in your mouth and in your heart, to do it.” Deuteronomy 30:14

      Mouth, heart, and action. In this verse, speaking, feeling, and thinking are named together — in Biblical Hebrew, the heart is understood to be the location of both feeling and thought — and then action is added as well. Earlier drafts of this sermon kept dropping one of these three terms in favor of the others, as if only one could matter at a time. The verse insists on keeping all of them, and then adds the verb that Wesley, Aristotle, and the Sinai story already put first: la'asoto, “to do it.” You do not need to wait for your feelings to resolve before you speak the words or take the action. The action itself shapes the feeling. You move out of your head and into practice, and the three — mouth, heart, and deed — come into alignment by being used together, not by being sorted out first in private.

      This is also why this work has to be communal rather than private. Meaningful religious experience is not built alone. It is built through shared practice: a minyan that exists only because you showed up as the tenth person, a meal brought to someone in mourning, a melody sung even when your voice breaks, words from the prayer book said before they feel like your own words. None of these actions is pretending to have a faith you lack. Each one is what actually produces that faith over time. The fixed structure of religious practice is what keeps the daily, uncertain effort of the grey area from turning into aimless confusion. Inside that structure, grief can move from one action to the next — toward belief, and eventually toward relief — without requiring you to invent your own meaning from nothing on your hardest day.

      At this point, the fear of not having enough loses its basis. We hesitate because we believe we are missing something: more faith, more healing, more answers, a purer motive, grief that has already been resolved. But this sense of lacking something is itself the mistake — the belief that real life can only start once everything is fully in place. The Torah reading says the opposite, and it says this to people who are already standing and already capable of acting.

      You are standing here today with everything you need to do what you are meant to do. Your doubts are part of what you have to work with. So is your grief. So are your breath and your hands. You do not need to be fully healed in order to be genuinely useful. The instruction is already close to you. It was close before you felt ready to notice it.

      From grief to relief

      To bring this together, return to where the sermon started.

      The happy-happy, joy-joy mood was a way of avoiding this exact confrontation. It kept our attention on unlimited options, so that obligation, mourning, and unfinished faith could be treated as inconveniences rather than as the real work in front of us. Yom Kippur asks us to look directly at that work instead. The place we were afraid of turns out to hold the same insight the Talmud calls a secret: act first, and understanding follows. Preach a faith until the preaching produces belief. Become a just person by doing just actions while you are still afraid to. Do the small, repeated acts — show up, bring the meal, say the prayer — and let those acts be the real steps from grief, to belief, to relief.

      The relief this sermon is describing is specific. It is what happens when you stop requiring a strong, certain feeling as a precondition for acting, and instead accept the day's plain requirements as sufficient guidance. Real joy is not the absence of fear and grief. It is the ability to keep acting while fear and grief are still present. A friend's death remains a death. A cousin's yahrzeit will come again next year. But the joy that can exist alongside these facts is more durable than the pleasant surface described at the start of this sermon, because it was built in the exact place that surface was designed to help us avoid.

      Stop managing how you appear. Face the fear that comes with obligation directly. Step into the plain, demanding work of practice — you are already standing in the middle of it — and trust the order that every source above describes: act, and belief follows the action. When the feeling finally comes, it will be relief.

      גְּמַר חֲתִימָה טוֹבָה. G'mar chatimah tovah. May you be sealed for a good year.

      Scholarly notes

      This is a sermon composed for 10 Tishrei 5787, not a study that happens to sound pastoral. The two losses are the speaker's, as first named: a friend's death, recent enough to carry into the room, and a cousin's yahrzeit. A later working draft replaced both with an empty chair and a parent's passing. That substitution is not used. No names, dates of death, or further circumstances are supplied here, because none were established.

      The short version is that sermon in the form in which it is spoken, and it is left in those words. It sets an empty chair at the holiday table beside the friend's death and the yahrzeit; it does not replace those two losses with a parent's passing. It also says “until” where Wesley's journal, quoted in the longer version, says “till.” The sections after it are the longer version. The notes and footnotes below attach to that longer text.

      Jackson's paper argues that Mary learns something new when she leaves the room, and concludes from this that physicalism (the view that physical facts are all the facts there are) is false. This sermon borrows the scene only as an image of experience that a complete theory cannot contain; it does not adopt Jackson's philosophical conclusion. The reversal used in the sermon — a congregation that needs to enter the black-and-white room rather than leave it — is a preaching device, not an interpretation of Jackson's argument. Similarly, the jazz chart illustrates the idea of constraint; it is not a claim about how music actually works. Musicians disagree about how strictly a chord chart should be followed. The image here only requires the simple fact that unlimited choice does not by itself produce music.

      Aristotle's distinction is worth stating carefully, because it would be easy to connect his argument to Jackson's incorrectly. Aristotle argues that the senses are not acquired through practice, while the virtues are. Jackson's Mary is about something different: what a complete physical description of vision still fails to capture. This sermon places lived faith on the virtue side of Aristotle's distinction, and uses Mary only to illustrate that having a theory about something is not the same as experiencing it. That connection between the two arguments is this sermon's construction, not something Aristotle himself made.

      The Hebrew word nishma in Exodus 24:7 literally means “we will hear.” The additional senses of “obey” and “understand” are both present in the verb's range of meaning and in later interpretation; treating nishma as “we will understand” is an interpretive gloss, and this sermon treats it as one rather than as a literal translation. Shabbat 88a is aggadic material — homiletic rather than legal — and its images of crowns, an apple tree, and a heavenly voice are meant as interpretation, not historical description; this sermon uses the same homiletic register. There is also a counter-tradition, found on the same Talmud page and in the Mekhilta, arguing that doing and understanding are not meant to be separated at all: one version has Moses telling the people that doing without understanding is not truly possible. This sermon follows the more famous teaching, which gives priority to na'aseh, doing, but does not claim that the other tradition does not exist.[9]

      The word “antinomian” is used here to name an instinct — the impulse to resist any binding practice — and its use is deliberately limited to that. It is not the name of a denomination, and it is not a claim about Reform Judaism as a historical movement. An earlier working draft of this sermon misspelled the word as “antinomial”; the intended contrast is with nomos, the Greek word for law.

      The discussion of the superego above depends on Freud's work and on the fuller account in The Divine Fracture. Applying the term “superego” to an entire congregation is an analogy, meant to describe a hesitation many people share. It does not diagnose anyone's individual psychological state, and it should not be preached or heard as though it did.
