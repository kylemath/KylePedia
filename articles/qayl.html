    Qayl
    Word قَيْل (qayl); Sabaic qyl, qwl, ql
    Other forms qayyil (قَيِّل); qaywil; miqwal (مِقْوَل); miqyal
    Plurals aqyāl, aqwāl, maqāwil, maqāwila, quyūl; Sabaic ʾqwl, ʾql
    Feminine qayla (قَيْلَة)
    The office al-qiyāla (القِيالة)
    Language Sabaic (Ancient South Arabian), borrowed into Arabic
    Sense in the inscriptions Member of the leading clan in a šʿb
    Sense in the lexica A king of Ḥimyar ranking below the supreme king
    Classical derivation From qawl (قَوْل), “speech, command”
    Epigraphic record Sabaic inscriptions in the Musnad script, among them Ry 507/1 and Jām 2867/10
    Type Comparative etymological essay, translated from Arabic

    Qayl (قَيْل; plural aqyāl and aqwāl) is the title of a South Arabian chief ranking below the king. The Arabic lexicographers and historians gloss it in one line — the qayl is the king below the supreme king, a ruler of Ḥimyar of the second rank[1] — and the inscriptions the word actually comes from are more particular than that. The standard dictionary of Sabaic files it under the root q-w-l and defines qyl as a member of the leading clan of a šʿb, the tribal and territorial community of ancient Yemen, recording alongside it a verb yqln, “be qayl over”, a noun mqwl for the qayl's residence, and mqwlt for his duties.[2]

    This entry is an imported study of where that word came from. Its author starts from a dissatisfaction: the classical derivation — qayl from qawl, “speech, command”, so that the qayl is the one whose word is carried out — fastens on a single sense of a root that has several, and Ibn Khaldūn was right to reject it while wrong about the reason.[3] In its place he assembles a wide dossier: Arabic roots resembling q-y-l that carry the senses of height, summit, precedence and choosing, and beside them Sumerian gal, “great”, Akkadian apkallu, and Egyptian qꜣ, “high”. The proposal is offered throughout as a possibility rather than a demonstration, and this edition keeps it that way: the argument and the section headings are the author's. What can be checked has been. The epigraphic attestations hold down to the line and number of the inscription; the one comparison with a documented loan history turns out to support something narrower than it is asked to; and the Sumerian and Canaanite comparisons do not survive checking at all. Each is set out in the scholarly notes with the text left standing as written.[4]

      Al-qayl and al-qawl

      The opening section is an inventory rather than an argument: the word, its variant vocalisations, the five plurals it takes, the abstract noun for the office, and the verb formed from it. Almost every form carries a dossier of quotations from the classical dictionaries and commentaries, and those dossiers are the notes.

      The qayl (according to the ancient Yemeni inscriptions) is the one who ranks next below the king. The word qayl has several forms, such as: qayl[1], qawl[5] (as in the Musnad script), miqwal[6], miqyal[7], and its origin has been traced back to qayyil[8] and qaywil[9]. Its plurals are: maqāwil[10], maqāwila[11], aqwāl[12], aqyāl[13], quyūl[14], and the noun al-qiyāla[15]. From it comes the verb (qāla) [“so-and-so qāla over us,” i.e., he ruled / “Glory to Him who donned might and qāla bihi,” i.e., ruled thereby and subdued][16], and taqayyala[17], and perhaps iqtāla[18]. The verb (qāla) also occurs in the inscriptions [“ʔšʕb yqlnn bnw mʕhr wḏḫwln / wʔšʕbhmw ʔšʕb yqln / wkl ql yqlnn”][19].
      القيل (حسب النقوش اليمنية القديمة) هو الذي بعد الملك. وكلمة قيل لها صيغ مثل: قيل، قَول (كما بالمساند)، مِقْوَل، مِقْيَل، وأُرْجِعَ الأصل إلى قيّل وقَيْوِل. وجمع الكلمة: مقاول، مقاولة، أقوال، أقيال، قيول، والاسم القيالة. وجاء منه الفعل (قال) [قال علينا فلان أي: مَلَكَ / سبحان الذي لبس العز وقال به، أي مَلَكَ به وقهر]، وتقيّل، وربما اقتال. وجاء الفعل (قال) بالنقوش [أشعب يقلنن بنو معهر وذخولن / وأشعبهمو أشعب يقلن / وكل قل يقلنن].

      The last of those forms is the one that ties the Arabic tradition to the stones. Where the lexicographers reconstruct qayl from a verb they already knew, the inscriptions have the verb itself, and the epigraphic dictionary glosses it in terms of the office rather than of speech. Setting the two columns of evidence beside each other shows where they agree and where the later tradition has filled a gap by analogy.

      The etymology of the word qayl

      Three derivations circulated among the Arabic philologists: from qawl, speech and command; from iḥtikām, adjudication; and from taqayyul, resembling — the qayl as the one who takes after the king before him. Ibn Khaldūn rejected all of them on the ground that Himyaritic was a different language and could not be run through Arabic morphology. The author's disagreement is narrow and worth following: he accepts the rejection and denies the reason for it.

      The philologists derived the word (qayl) from al-qawl (speech and command)[20]. Some derived it from arbitration [“the one below the king was called qayl because he adjudicates and his ruling is carried out”][21] — and this is closer, for it lies between the sense of “speech” and the sense of “king” and “ruler.” Others derived the word from al-taqayyul[22], i.e., resemblance (as if taqayyala were a variant of taqayyaḍa), as though he resembles the king.
      اشتق أهل اللغة كلمة (قَيْل) من القول (الكلام والأمر)، وبعضهم جعلها من الاحتكام [قيل لمن دون الملك قيل لأنه يحتكم فيمضى حكمه] وهذا أقرب، فهو بين معنى الكلام ومعنى الملك والحاكم، وبعضهم جعل الكلمة من التقيّل التشبّه (كأن تقيّل لغة في تقيّض)، كأنه يشبه الملك.
      Ibn Khaldūn opposed these derivations [“some claim, concerning the derivation of qayl in the Himyaritic tongue, that it is from al-qawl ... but that is not correct”]. Yet the reason is not what Ibn Khaldūn supposed[23].
      وعارض ابن خلدون هذه الاشتقاقات [يزعم بعضهم في اشتقاق القيل في اللّسان الحميريّ أنّه من القول ... وليس ذلك بصحيح]. وليس السبب ما ظنّ ابن خلدون.
      I think the error lies in restricting oneself to only some of the meanings when deriving the word qayl. One ought rather to examine the remaining possibilities and meanings of the root q-y-l, q-w-l, and its cognates (such as: q-l-l, q-l-y, q-ʿ-l, q-n-n, j-l-l, kh-y-r, kh-l-l, w-q-l, w-j-l, gh-l-w, ...). For the Himyaritic word qayl conveys the sense of (head[24], chief, leader[25], king, foremost one, great one, ...); and had we searched the root q-w-l and its cognates for meanings such as [first, beginning, head, high[26], great, mighty, best, exalted, chosen, foremost, ...], we might have come closer to the etymology of the word qayl.
      وأظن الخطأ هو الاقتصار على بعض المعاني في اشتقاق كلمة قَيْل. وكان ينبغي النظر في بقية الاحتمالات والمعاني لجذر قَيْل وقَوْل وأشباهه (مثل: قلل، قلى، قعل، قنن، جلل، خير، خلل، وقل، وجل، غلا، ...). فكلمة قَيْل الحميرية تفيد معنى (رأس، رئيس، قائد، ملك، مُقَدّم، كبير، ...)، ولو بحثنا في جذر قول وأشباهه عن معانٍ مثل [أول، بداية، رأس، عالٍ، كبير، عظيم، خير، جليل، مختار، قدّام، ...]، لربما اقتربنا أكثر من اشتقاق كلمة قَيْل.

      Meanings in the root qyl and its cognates

      What follows is the evidence for that proposal: five senses drawn from q-l-l, q-l-y, q-ʿ-l, q-n-n and their neighbours, each glossed out of the dictionaries and each chosen because it would suit a word for a chief. The source sets every sense under its own subheading; here each subheading opens the paragraph it governs, so that the run of them reads as one list.

      The sense of elevation and rising. qalla: (to raise)[27], (to rise high)[28] / qalla [noun] (a rising, recovery)[29] / aqalla: (to lift, raise)[30] / istaqalla: (to rise, ascend)[31] / taqālla: (to rise high, tower)[32] / iqlawlā: (to rise, ascend)[33] / aqāla (to raise?)[34] / qilāl (props for raising)[35] / qiyāl (name of a high mountain)[36].
      معنى العلو والارتفاع: قلّ: (رفع)، (علا) / قَلّة (نهضة) / أقلّ: (رفع) / استقلّ: (ارتفع) / تقالّ: (ارتفع، تعالى) / اقلولى: (ارتفع، علا) / أقال (رفع؟) / قِلال (للرفع) / قِيال (اسم جبل عالٍ).
      The sense of head / summit. qalā (he struck him on the head)[37] / qulla (head, top)[38], qunna (height, summit)[39] / qulan (heads, summits)[40] / qaʿl (mountaintop)[41], like qulla[42].
      معنى الرأس: قلى (ضربه على رأسه) / قُلّة (رأس، أعلى)، قُنّة (علوّ) / قُلى (رؤوس) / قعل (رأس جبل)، مثل قُلّة.
      The sense of singling out and choosing. qāla (he singled out, appropriated)[43] / iqtāla (he chose)[44].
      معنى الاختصاص والاختيار: قال (اختصّ) / اقتال (اختار).
      The sense of precedence and beginning. qāla (beginning)[45] / iqlawlā: to go forward, advance[46].
      معنى التقدّم والابتداء: قال (ابتداء) / اقلولى: تقدّم.
      The sense of the face. qayhal: (face)[47].
      معنى الوجه: قيهل: (وجه).

      Words resembling qayl in the ancient languages of the region

      The comparison now widens past Arabic. Three languages are brought in, and they are not brought in on the same footing: Sumerian and Akkadian supply a word of the right shape and sense, Canaanite supplies a proper name, and Egyptian supplies a dictionary entry. The evidence for each is of a different kind, and the scholarly notes separate them.

      Sumerian and Akkadian

      The clearest ancient word resembling (qayl) is the Sumerian word (gal), whose pronunciation resembles (qāl, ghāl, kāl, ...) and whose meaning is (great, big). The Sumerian king was called lugal (lu = man, gal = great).
      أوضح كلمة قديمة تشبه (قَيْل) هي كلمة (gal) السومرية والتي يشبه نطقها (قال، غال، كال، ...) ومعناها (عظيم، كبير). فقد كان الملك السومري يسمى لوقال (لو = رجل، قال = كبير).
      Similar to it is the well-known word (haykal), whose Sumerian origin is (e-gal), meaning “great house” (e = house, gal = great). In Arabic the word haykal came to convey (hugeness, grandeur, length, and height)[48], perhaps because of its second syllable (kal), which resembles (qayl). And (haykal) may have been pronounced in some dialects as (hiql)[49], which is closer to the word (qayl).
      ومثله الكلمة الشهيرة (هيكل) التي أصلها السومري (إي كال)، بمعنى بيت كبير (إي = بيت، كال = كبير). وكلمة هيكل أصبحت في العربية تفيد (الضخامة والعظمة والطول والارتفاع)، ولعل السبب هو مقطعها الثاني (كَل) الذي يشبه (قيل). و(هيكل) ربما لفظته بعض اللهجات بصيغة (هِقْل)، وهذا أقرب لكلمة (قيل).
      The Akkadian word (apkal), meaning (sage, priest), is from the Sumerian origin (ab-gal), i.e., “great water,” or (abba-gal), where abba means (father, elder, aged one). The word apkal appeared in ancient Arabian inscriptions with the meaning “priest”[50].
      وكلمة (أفكل) الأكدية التي تعني (حكيم، كاهن)، من أصل سومري (أب كال) أي ماء عظيم أو (أبّا كال) حيث أبّا (أب، شيخ، مسنّ، عجوز). وظهرت كلمة أفكل في نقوش عربية قديمة بمعنى كاهن.

      Canaanite

      The name (Jālūt / Goliath) mentioned in the Qur'an may share the same origin as the Sumerian (gal, kal) and the Arabic (qayl). Among the possible meanings of Jālūt are: the great (the exalted)[51], the prominent[52], the chosen — and if that is correct, these meanings are not far from the meanings of qayl.
      قد يكون اسم (جالوت) المذكور بالقرآن، من نفس أصل (قال، كال) السومرية و(قَيْل) العربية. ومن احتمالات معنى جالوت: العظيم (الجليل)، البارز، المختار، فإن صحّ ذلك فهذه المعاني غير بعيدة عن معاني القيل.

      Ancient Egyptian

      In Ancient Egyptian there is the word (qa), meaning elevation, rising, and grandeur, which is close to the word (qayl):
      وبالمصرية القديمة كلمة (قا) التي بمعنى العلو والارتفاع والعظمة، وهي قريبة من كلمة (قيل):
      qa — elevation, rising, grandeur Wallis Budge, An Egyptian Hieroglyphic Dictionary, 760, as cited and glossed in the source edition[53]

      Arabic words close in form and meaning to qayl

      The closing section returns to Arabic and widens the net a second time, to roots beginning with j, kh, gh, k and w that carry senses of bulk, greatness, elevation, or selection. The last paragraph states the thesis in its strongest form and, characteristically, in the conditional: that the qayl may be the chosen one, that khayr may be a variant of qayl, and that all these roots may go back to one origin.

      There exist Arabic words not far from the form and meaning of the Sumerian gal and (qayl, qawl), such as:
      توجد كلمات عربية غير بعيدة عن لفظ ومعنى جال (gal) السومرية و(قيل، قول)، مثل:
      (jayʾal, jayʾal, jayhal, jayḥal)[54], meaning (huge, great), and like it (jaḥl)[55]. The words (jall) and (jalīl)[56] carry the sense of grandeur. There are other similar words, such as: wajala (to grow great)[57], wujūl (elders, chiefs)[58], waqala[59] (to rise, ascend), kayyūl[60] (that which is elevated of the ground), kalla[61] (to rise; the chest; going forward), khayl[62] (to be great, huge — pride), ghalā[63] (to rise, grow great), kahl[64] (to rise, grow tall), kahara[65] (to rise), jaʾara[66] (to grow tall, rise). These preceding roots may all stem from the same origin as the root of the word qayl.
      (جَيْئَل، جَيْأَل، جَيْهَلٌ، جيحل) ومعناها (ضخم، عظيم)، ومثله (جحل). ولفظ (جلّ) و(جليل) من معنى العظمة. وهناك كلمات أخرى مشابهة مثل: وجل (كبر)، وجول (شيوخ)، وقل (علا، صعد)، كيول (ما أشرف)، كلل (علا، الصدر، التقدم)، خيل (كبر، ضخم)، غلا (ارتفع، عظم)، كهل (ارتفع، طال)، كهر (ارتفع)، جأر (طال، ارتفع). فهذه الجذور السابقة قد تكون من نفس أصل جذر كلمة قَيْل.
      In addition to the meanings of chieftaincy (head), elevation, grandeur, greatness, precedence, and foremost rank, it is possible that qayl derives from the sense of choosing, selection, and singling out — like the verb qāla[67] and iqtāla[68], which mean khāra (to prefer, select, choose)[69] and ikhtāra, and jāla[70] (to choose), ijtāla, and jaljala[71], and perhaps (khalla)[72]. Thus the qayl may be the one whom the supreme king chooses (iqtāla = chooses)[73], or the one whom his people choose. Al-iqtiyāl means choosing (al-Jīm: al-iqtiyāl means al-ikhtiyār, choosing). If this possibility is correct, then the qayl (or “qayl of the people”) may mean the “best” (khayr) of the people — as though khayr were a variant of qayl, and khayyir[74] a variant of qayyil. Or, more generally, the root of the word qayl and the remaining roots we have mentioned may all stem from one and the same, or a closely related, linguistic origin.
      وبالإضافة إلى معاني الترؤّس (رأس) والعلو والعظمة والكبر والصدارة والتقدم، قد يحتمل أن يكون القيل من معنى الاختيار والاصطفاء والاختصاص، مثل فعل قال واقتال، الذي بمعنى خار (فضّل، انتقى، اصطفى) واختار، وجال (اختار) واجتال وجلجل، وربما (خلّ). فقد يكون القيل هو الذي يقتاله (يختاره) الملك الأعظم أو الذي يقتاله قومه. والاقتيال هو الاختيار (الجيم: الاقْتِيالَ: الاختيار). فإن صحّ هذا الاحتمال، فالقَيْل (أو قَيْل القوم) قد يكون بمعنى (خَيْر) القوم، وكأن خَيْر لغة في قَيْل، وخيّر لغة في قيّل. أو بشكل عام، قد يكون جذر كلمة قَيْل وبقية الجذور التي ذكرناها، من أصل لغوي واحد أو متقارب.

      Scholarly notes

      The argument here proceeds by resemblance: a word is set beside other words that sound like it and mean something compatible, and the accumulation is offered as a reason to prefer one etymology over another. That is a legitimate way to generate a hypothesis and not a way to confirm one, and the author is careful about the difference — the governing verbs are aẓunn (“I think”), rubbamā (“perhaps”), qad yakūn (“it may be”). The text is left as written. What follows is what could be checked, and how it came out. The source asserts no arithmetic, so the usual recomputation had nothing to work on; the checkable material is lexical and epigraphic instead.

      The epigraphic claims hold, and hold precisely. The word is genuinely South Arabian, not an Arabic formation applied retrospectively to Yemen. The Sabaic Dictionary records the noun in three writings with two plurals, a verb, a noun for the qayl's residence and another for his duties, and the inscription it cites for the verb yqln is Jām 2867 — the same inscription the source quotes for that form. RES 3945, cited for ʾfklt, is one of the two long texts of Karibʾil Watar from Ṣirwāḥ, and the dictionary glosses the word there as “priest in conquered Nashan”. Two of the three sigla in the source therefore check out at the line; the Wādī Ḥarīr and Sar-Ḥāz readings have not been verified against the corpus.[75]

      The definition is narrower in the stones than in the lexica. Where the Arabic tradition says “the king below the supreme king”, the epigraphic definition is “member of the leading clan in a šʿb”. The difference is not a quibble: the first describes a rank in a monarchy, the second an aristocratic standing within a tribal community, which is how the office is read in the modern literature on South Arabian kingship.[76] The Arabic gloss is what the institution looked like to writers who knew Ḥimyar only in its last centuries, and it is the gloss the source's opening line inherits. Nothing in the argument depends on which is right, but the reader should know that the two columns of the table above are not two witnesses to one definition.

      Sumerian gal is a real word and cannot be a cognate. The analysis of lugal as lú “man” plus gal “great” is correct and standard. But Sumerian has no established relatives; it is not a Semitic language, so a shared root with qayl is not available even in principle, and the only possible relation is borrowing. Borrowing would have to be shown, not inferred from resemblance, and g answering Arabic q is not a correspondence anyone has established between the two.[77] The resemblance is real; what it is evidence of is another matter.

      Haykal is a genuine loan, and it travels as a whole word. This is the one comparison in the section with a documented history, and it is worth stating exactly, because the history is not quite the one the source proposes. Sumerian é.gal, “big house”, was borrowed into Akkadian as ekallu and from Akkadian into the west, giving Ugaritic hkl, Hebrew hêkāl, Aramaic hēkəlā and finally Arabic haykal.[78] So the -kal the source hears inside haykal really is Sumerian gal. But it arrived inside a compound that was borrowed intact, which is the opposite of evidence that Arabic contains a free morpheme kal ~ qayl meaning “great”. The Arabic senses of hugeness and height that the source's note 44 documents at length are a later development within Arabic from “palace, great building”, not an inheritance of the Sumerian element. Apkallu is a loan from Sumerian abgal, written NUN.ME, and its second element is indeed gal; the first is unsettled, and the gloss “great water” is not the standard reading. The Sabaic ʾfkl of RES 3945 is in turn a loan from the Akkadian, which is how the dictionary marks it.[79]

      Jālūt almost certainly does not belong here. Goliath is usually taken as a Philistine name of Greek or Anatolian origin rather than a Semitic one, compared with Lydian forms such as Alyattes; two names of that shape, ʾlwt and wlt, turned up on a tenth- or ninth-century sherd at Gath, which is the city the biblical story gives him.[80] The Arabic Jālūt is a transmitted form of that name, not an independent Semitic word, so it cannot be evidence for a root shared with qayl. The source offers the connection as a possibility and conditions it — “if that is correct” — and it is the one comparison in the entry that can be answered rather than merely doubted.

      What the whole dossier does and does not establish. The roots gathered in the last two sections differ from one another in their first radical (q, j, kh, gh, k, w) and often in their second, and it is exactly those differences that a demonstration of common origin would have to explain by regular correspondence rather than set aside.[81] Two roots that mean “be great” and share one consonant are not thereby related; Semitic is full of such pairs. The observation the source makes in its note 58, that ʿalā and ghalā both mean “to rise”, is a case in point: ʿayn and ghayn do interchange in some words, but the two verbs are ordinarily kept apart as distinct roots. Read as a claim about descent, the dossier does not carry. Read as what it says it is — a demonstration that the semantic field around qayl is crowded with height, precedence and choosing, and that the classical derivation from “speech” picked one thread out of that field and called it the origin — it is a real observation, and it survives everything above.
