    Devarim–dvorim wordplay

    This entry is about the Hebrew wordplay connecting Deuteronomy and bees. For the biblical book, see Book of Deuteronomy on Wikipedia.
    Entry Sources Tags
    Read Collection Contents
    Devarim–dvorim wordplay
    Also known as דברים / דבורים midrashic pun
    Language Biblical and Rabbinic Hebrew
    Primary text Deuteronomy (Heb. Devarim)
    Key terms devarim (matters / uttered words); milim (lexical words, Mod. Heb.); ledaber (to speak, root d-b-r); dvorim / devorim (bees)
    Hebrew דברים · דבורים
    Type Paronomasia and orthographic wordplay; midrashic derash
    Related names Deborah (דבורה, “bee”)

    The Devarim–devorim wordplay is a rabbinic interpretation of the similarity between devarim (דְּבָרִים, “words” or “matters”)—the Hebrew title of the Book of Deuteronomy—and devorim (דְּבוֹרִים, “bees”). Devarim Rabbah reads the opening words of the book through this resemblance and compares Torah to a bee whose honey is sweet and whose sting is painful. The comparison is midrashic rather than etymological: it produces an interpretation from similar sounds and letters without requiring the two nouns to have the same historical origin.[1]

    The same chapter describes the Amorites pursuing Israel “as bees do” (Deuteronomy 1:44), a verse in which the unpointed consonants of “the bees” are identical to those of “the words” in 1:1. That coincidence of spelling, the hortatory style of the book, and a long reception in Jewish and Christian commentary have all been drawn into the comparison, as has the unrelated English idiom of the spelling bee.

      Linguistic background

      In Hebrew, davar (דבר) means “word,” “thing,” or “matter”; its plural is devarim (דברים). The book commonly called Deuteronomy in English is named in Hebrew for its opening phrase, Eleh ha-devarim (אלה הדברים)—“These are the words”—which introduces the discourses attributed to Moses. The verb diber (“he spoke”) and the noun dibbur (“speech”) belong to the same d-b-r word family. [2]

      In Modern Hebrew, a countable lexical item is usually a milah (מילה), plural milim (מילים). Davar more often denotes a thing or matter, while devarim can also denote the content of an utterance. A speaker may therefore count the milim in a sentence but summarize the devarim delivered in a speech. These semantic ranges overlap, and context rather than a rigid rule determines the preferred noun. [3]

      The modern distinction does not map neatly onto earlier Hebrew and Aramaic. Millah occurs in biblical poetry, including Psalm 139:4 and 2 Samuel 23:2, and is frequent in Job. Biblical Aramaic uses millin for “words” (Daniel 7:25), while Talmudic Aramaic milta (מילתא) commonly means “matter” or “case.” The title Devarim reflects biblical prose usage and the conventional practice of naming a Torah book from a word near its beginning; it does not establish a permanent opposition between davar and milah. [4]

      The word for “bee” is devorah (דבורה), plural devorim (דבורים). The pointed forms devarim and devorim are close but not identical in pronunciation, differing in their second vowel. Lexicons record competing proposals for the origin of devorah, none of which requires descent from the speech root d-b-r. [5]

      The link between the two words is therefore one of sound and spelling rather than derivation. This costs the midrash nothing, since it never claims to report an etymology; it hears one word in another and builds a lesson on what it hears. Students of rabbinic interpretation treat such readings as deliberate wordplay rather than mistaken philology, and the distinction matters only when a later writer converts the homily into a claim about the history of the language.[6]

      Rabbinic interpretation regularly draws meaning from sound and spelling. The Babylonian Talmud employs the formula al tikrei (“do not read X but Y”), as at Berakhot 64a, where banayikh (“your sons”) is reread as bonayikh (“your builders”), and it uses notarikon at Shabbat 105a. Such techniques explain how a midrash can hear “bees” in “words” without claiming that the Masoretic vocalization should be changed in ordinary reading.[7]

      A similar interpretive association joins midbar (“wilderness”) to medabber (“speaking”). Historical lexicons usually relate the wilderness noun to an open pasture, possibly a place to which livestock are driven, rather than directly to verbal speech. Later homilies nevertheless use the shared consonants, especially where Hosea 2:16 (2:14 in many English versions) places wilderness and speech in a single verse. [5]

      Classical midrashic interpretation

      The direct source for the wordplay is Devarim Rabbah 1:6 (numbered 1:5 in some editions). Commenting on eleh ha-devarim, it offers several bee comparisons. Israel follows the righteous and the prophets as bees follow their leader; a bee’s honey is sweet and its sting bitter, as Torah gives life to those who keep it and punishment to those who transgress it; and a bee gathers for its owner as Israel accumulates good deeds for God. These are separate interpretations collected under the same opening phrase, not a single sustained allegory. [1]

      מה הדבורה הזו דבשה מתוק ועוקצה מר — כך הן דברי תורה. Mah ha-devorah ha-zo, divshah matok ve-uktzah mar — kakh hen divrei Torah. “Just as this bee’s honey is sweet and its sting bitter, so are the words of Torah.” Devarim Rabbah 1:6

      A further interpretation in the same passage is more pointed. Because a bee’s honey goes to its owner while its sting is for others, that version calls Torah “an elixir of life for Israel and a potion of death for idolaters.” The contrast here is communal rather than a matter of observance, and it is polemical as well as pedagogical: the same image that promises sweetness to insiders assigns the sting to outsiders. Later preaching on the bee inherits a related difficulty whenever it treats hierarchy or painful rebuke as self-evidently instructive. These are the passages as they stand; the moral weight readers give them is another matter. [1]

      The same chapter also treats Moses as a speaker. In Exodus 4:10 he protests lo ish devarim anokhi, “I am not a man of words,” and Devarim Rabbah 1:1 asks how such a man came to deliver a book of devarim, answering that his tongue was healed. A parable attributed to R. Tanchuma in the following section compares him to a merchant of purple cloth: what is precious among buyers is nothing before the king, so Moses was no man of words before God yet spoke devarim to Israel. These interpretations concern the speaker rather than bees, but they draw on the same title word.[8]

      Other rabbinic texts provide parallels without commenting directly on the devarim–devorim resemblance. At Yoma 72b Torah is said to become “an elixir of life” (sam chayim) for one who is worthy and “a deadly potion” (sam ha-mavet) for one who is not; Taanit 7a develops a related comparison between Torah and water. The same elixir-and-potion pair therefore appears in three configurations: keeping against transgressing the commandments, Israel against idolaters, and the worthy against the unworthy student. The Talmudic passages make no mention of bees.[9]

      Biblical images of sweetness and sharp instruction form a wider interpretive setting. Psalms 19:11 and 119:103 describe divine instruction as sweeter than honey. Song of Songs 4:11, “honey and milk are under your tongue,” is applied to Torah in Song of Songs Rabbah, while Ezekiel finds a divine scroll “as sweet as honey” in his mouth (Ezekiel 3:3). Ecclesiastes 12:11 declares that “the words of the sages are like goads” (divrei chakhamim ke-darvonot), and Chagigah 3b expounds the goad as speech that pricks the hearer toward life and directs the wayward, as a herdsman directs an animal.

      Sifrei Devarim 306, commenting on Moses’ image—“may my teaching drop like rain” (Deuteronomy 32:2)—collects several interpretations of the rain and dew figures; some stress gentle nourishment, others abundance or force. Medieval Ashkenazic initiation rites, recorded in sources including Machzor Vitry, had a child taste honey associated with written letters. The practice enacted the biblical sweetness metaphor, although it was not based specifically on the title of Deuteronomy. [10]

      A separate halakhic discussion concerns the permissibility of bee honey even though the insect itself is not kosher. The Talmud at Bekhorot 7b records both a biological rationale—that bees bring the material into their bodies without producing it from their own substance—and a scriptural rationale for treating honey as an exception. Maimonides codifies the former (Mishneh Torah, Forbidden Foods 3:3). Later sermons may connect that ruling to the honey-and-sting motif, but the Talmudic passage itself is legal rather than an interpretation of Devarim.[11]

      Deuteronomy as speech

      Deuteronomy presents most of its contents as speeches delivered by Moses before Israel enters the land. The narration repeatedly marks shifts between address and reported action, and the book emphasizes hearing, teaching, remembering, writing, and public recitation. Its Hebrew title therefore identifies both the opening words and the book’s dominant literary form.

      Sifrei Devarim, Piska 1, followed by Rashi, reads eleh ha-devarim as divrei tokhachah, “words of rebuke,” and decodes the place-names of the opening verse as allusions to Israel’s failures. On this reading Moses rebukes by allusion rather than by immediately naming each offence. The same interpretation takes “to all Israel” to mean that the rebuke was public and could not later be disputed by those who were absent. [12]

      Jewish sources give different accounts of the relation between Moses’ voice and divine revelation. At Megillah 31b, Abaye distinguishes the curses of Leviticus 26, spoken in the plural and uttered “from the mouth of the Almighty,” from those of Deuteronomy 28, framed in the singular and said by Moses mi-pi atzmo, “of his own mouth.” Nachmanides’ introduction describes the book as prophecy restated for the generation entering the land. The Zohar later interprets Moses’ voice as united with divine power. What is at issue in each case is the mode of revelation, not whether the book is Torah.[13]

      When the book was written is a separate question. Most modern critical scholarship places a core of Deuteronomy in late monarchic Judah, connects its promulgation with the reform of Josiah, and reads it as the opening of a larger Deuteronomistic history; traditional and conservative scholars defend an earlier setting and dispute that reconstruction. The wordplay on devarim survives under either account, because it belongs to the reception of the book rather than to its composition. An argument that the resemblance was intended by whoever wrote Deuteronomy 1:44 is only as strong as the account of authorship behind it.[14]

      Deuteronomy 1:5 says that Moses “began to expound (be’er) this Torah”; Rashi, drawing on Midrash Tanchuma, explains that he expounded it in seventy languages, and the theme of Torah rendered in seventy tongues recurs in the Talmud’s discussion of the inscribed stones at Sotah 32a and 35b–36a. This tradition treats “expounding” as making Torah publicly intelligible across languages, not merely repeating an earlier text.

      Opening and closing frames

      The book opens with devarim as its second word. The verse is dense in related sounds: ha-devarim (“the words”) and diber (“spoke”) belong to the same word family, while ba-midbar (“in the wilderness”) repeats the consonants. The last resemblance supports later homily but is not necessarily a derivation from the speech sense of d-b-r.

      אֵ֣לֶּה הַדְּבָרִ֗ים אֲשֶׁ֨ר דִּבֶּ֤ר מֹשֶׁה֙ אֶל־כׇּל־יִשְׂרָאֵ֔ל בְּעֵ֖בֶר הַיַּרְדֵּ֑ן בַּמִּדְבָּ֡ר בָּֽעֲרָבָה֩ מ֨וֹל ס֜וּף בֵּֽין־פָּארָ֧ן וּבֵֽין־תֹּ֛פֶל וְלָבָ֥ן וַחֲצֵרֹ֖ת וְדִ֥י זָהָֽב׃ Eleh ha-devarim asher diber Mosheh el kol Yisrael be-ever ha-Yarden ba-midbar, ba-Aravah mol Suf, bein Paran u-vein Tofel ve-Lavan va-Chatzerot ve-Di Zahav. “These are the words that Moses spoke to all Israel beyond the Jordan, in the wilderness, in the Arabah opposite Suph, between Paran and Tophel, Laban, Hazeroth and Di-zahab.” Deuteronomy 1:1 (emphasis added)

      The book’s final verses return to Moses and to the audience named at the beginning, not to bees. Deuteronomy 1:1 introduces words spoken “to all Israel” (el kol Yisrael), while 34:12 ends with deeds performed “before the eyes of all Israel” (le-einei kol Yisrael). The repetition works as an inclusio, framing Israel as the audience and witness of both the speech and the deeds, and it is the strongest structural echo the book offers.[15]

      וְלֹא־קָ֨ם נָבִ֥יא ע֛וֹד בְּיִשְׂרָאֵ֖ל כְּמֹשֶׁ֑ה אֲשֶׁר֙ יְדָע֣וֹ יְהֹוָ֔ה פָּנִ֖ים אֶל־פָּנִֽים׃ … וּלְכֹל֙ הַיָּ֣ד הַחֲזָקָ֔ה וּלְכֹ֖ל הַמּוֹרָ֣א הַגָּד֑וֹל אֲשֶׁר֙ עָשָׂ֣ה מֹשֶׁ֔ה לְעֵינֵ֖י כׇּל־יִשְׂרָאֵֽל׃ Ve-lo kam navi od be-Yisrael ke-Mosheh, asher yeda‘o Adonai panim el panim … u-le-khol ha-yad ha-chazakah u-le-khol ha-mora ha-gadol asher asah Mosheh le-einei kol Yisrael. “Never again did there arise in Israel a prophet like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face … and for all the mighty hand and awesome power that Moses displayed before the eyes of all Israel.” Deuteronomy 34:10, 12 (emphasis added)

      The bee verse has sometimes been drawn into this frame as well, on the grounds that it stands near the end of the first chapter. It does not belong there. Verses 45 and 46 follow it, the speech runs on into chapter 2, and the chapter division is a medieval convenience rather than a seam in the Hebrew. What Deuteronomy 1:44 offers is not structure but spelling.

      Bees within Devarim (Deuteronomy 1:44)

      Deuteronomy 1:44 recounts Israel’s defeat after the people attempt to enter the land despite a divine warning. The Amorites pursue them “as bees do,” the only occurrence of the plural “bees” in the Pentateuch. Read as narrative the simile is entirely self-sufficient: the Amorites came down in numbers and harried a routed army.[16]

      וַיֵּצֵ֨א הָאֱמֹרִ֜י הַיֹּשֵׁ֨ב בָּהָ֤ר הַהוּא֙ לִקְרַאתְכֶ֔ם וַיִּרְדְּפ֣וּ אֶתְכֶ֔ם כַּאֲשֶׁ֥ר תַּעֲשֶׂ֖ינָה הַדְּבֹרִ֑ים וַֽיַּכְּת֥וּ אֶתְכֶ֛ם בְּשֵׂעִ֖יר עַד־חׇרְמָֽה׃ Va-yetzei ha-Emori ha-yoshev ba-har ha-hu likratchem, va-yirdefu etchem ka-asher ta’aseinah ha-devorim, va-yaketu etchem be-Se‘ir ad Chormah. “Then the Amorites who dwelt in that hill country came out against you and pursued you as bees do, and crushed you at Seir as far as Hormah.” Deuteronomy 1:44 (emphasis added)

      In the Masoretic text, “the bees” in 1:44 is written without a consonantal vav marking the o vowel: הַדְּבֹרִים. Its consonants are therefore identical to those of “the words” in 1:1, הַדְּבָרִים. The Masoretic vowel points distinguish the words, but a Torah scroll records only the consonantal text. “Defective” is a technical description of the spelling, not a claim that a scribe made an error.[17]

      Two things follow, and a third does not. Biblical Hebrew omits vowel letters constantly, so a defective spelling here is unremarkable in itself, and the two words stay distinct in every vocalized reading. What the scroll produces is an opening in the written text that the midrash was free to use. It is not evidence that the writer of 1:44 was playing on the first verse of the book; the identity is real, and opportunistic.

      The medieval commentators fasten on different features of the simile. Rashi has the Amorites die after striking Israel, as a bee dies after stinging; his reading depends on a familiar fact about worker honeybees, whose barbed sting lodges in elastic skin and tears free when the insect pulls away. It is not a general rule about bees, and the verse itself says only that Israel was routed. Ibn Ezra takes the point to be pursuit once a hive is disturbed, and Chizkuni the way one bee leaving draws the rest after it. Each is a plausible observation; the Hebrew verb “pursued” requires none of them.[18] Elsewhere in Scripture the figure serves the same military purpose: Psalm 118:12 compares surrounding enemies to bees, and Isaiah 7:18 has God summon “the bee that is in the land of Assyria.”

      Judges 14:8–18 supplies a different bee image: Samson finds honey in a lion’s carcass and asks a riddle whose answer turns on sweetness emerging from strength. This passage and the hostile swarms of Deuteronomy and Psalms provide biblical precedents for the contrasting qualities that Devarim Rabbah later brings together.

      Adam Clarke’s nineteenth-century commentary reads “as bees do” as “by irresistible numbers.” Elsewhere he compares biblical swarms with Homer and Virgil and repeats an ancient explanation that beekeepers summoned bees by whistling. The Hebrew wordplay goes unmentioned; his interest is in military imagery, classical parallels and natural history. He also judged Deuteronomy 1:1–5 to be a later addition, “probably either by Joshua or Ezra,” which puts a nineteenth-century Methodist commentator on the same ground as the compositional questions raised above.[19]

      Retelling earlier Torah and rhetorical style

      Deuteronomy retells events and laws found in Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers, but seldom repeats them verbatim. The Decalogue, the appointment of judges, the episode of the spies, festival law, and rules of worship and justice reappear with changes in wording, sequence, explanation, or emphasis. Traditional commentary reconciles the versions as one revelation differently reported; critical scholarship reads them as successive formulations, with Deuteronomy reworking material it inherited.[20]

      The best-known case is the Decalogue, where Exodus 20:8 commands “remember” (zakhor) the sabbath day and Deuteronomy 5:12 commands “keep” (shamor) it. Rabbinic tradition harmonizes the divergence by asserting that “zakhor and shamor were uttered in a single utterance,” a formulation found at Rosh Hashanah 27a and Shevuot 20b and applied halakhically at Berakhot 20b. Deuteronomy also grounds sabbath observance in Israel’s release from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15), whereas Exodus 20:11 appeals to creation. Two motives for one commandment can be taken as complementary theology or as a trace of successive legal drafting; the wording differs by more than a single verb in either case.[21]

      The spies present the same problem in narrative form. Numbers 13 has the mission commanded by God; Deuteronomy 1:22 has it proposed by the people. Rashi harmonizes the two as stages of a single event and reads the Deuteronomic wording as a sign of the people’s disorder, crowding round Moses rather than approaching through their elders. Read critically, they are two accounts of one tradition, told to different ends.

      Ibn Ezra’s long commentary on Exodus 20:1 compares words to bodies and meanings to souls, arguing that wording can change while meaning remains. Nachmanides explains Deuteronomy as commandments restated with admonition for the generation entering the land. Abarbanel emphasizes its oratorical setting, while Malbim and the Netziv attend closely to differences in phrasing. All of them take variation to be meaningful; they part company over whether the two wordings deliver precisely the same content.[22]

      Deuteronomy’s prose is characteristically hortatory. Commands are frequently accompanied by motives based on the exodus, the gift of the land, covenant loyalty, or concern for vulnerable people. Recurrent words include “hear,” “remember,” “forget,” “love,” “fear,” “cling,” “heart,” and “soul.” This marks it off from the concentrated ritual terminology of Leviticus and the census, itinerary, and tribal vocabulary of Numbers.

      The difference is one of genre, not of quality, and it will not support the claim that Deuteronomy is the most linguistically complex book of the Torah. Leviticus commands a far more differentiated technical vocabulary; Numbers handles administrative and geographical detail that Deuteronomy never attempts; Genesis and Exodus sustain narrative and poetry of their own. Lexical variety, the incidence of rare words, syntactic depth and ease of recitation do not rank the books in the same order. That Deuteronomy is the most rhetorically developed of the five is a defensible judgement, and a narrower one than it is usually made to carry.[23]

      The book uses extended conditional sentences and repeated purpose clauses introduced by lema’an (“in order that”) and warnings introduced by pen (“lest”). It also poses rhetorical questions, as in Deuteronomy 4:7–8 and 4:32–34. A frequently discussed feature, known as Numeruswechsel, is its abrupt alternation between singular and plural second-person address. This has been explained as a trace of composition and redaction, as a movement between the individual and the assembled people, and as ordinary rhetorical variation.[24]

      Deuteronomy’s language of oath, blessing, curse, loyalty, and exclusive allegiance has been compared with ancient Near Eastern treaties, especially Neo-Assyrian loyalty oaths. The comparison is influential but debated, because treaty language was used across periods and settings. Chapter 32 stands apart from the surrounding prose: its poetry carries rare vocabulary and difficult syntax, and its date and linguistic profile are disputed. Those difficulties belong to the poem rather than to the book’s prose.[24]

      Sound patterning and scribal form

      Deuteronomy repeats sounds and word forms conspicuously. The first verse sets ha-devarim, diber and ba-midbar within a few words of one another and then runs through a string of place-names; 1:44, ka-asher ta’aseinah ha-devorim, crowds sibilants and gutturals into a short clause. Whether such lines are hard to say is a question about readers rather than about the text. Pronunciation differs between reconstructions of ancient Hebrew and the modern reading traditions, and the vocalization and cantillation of the Masoretic text record a received performance, not the sound of the verses when they were composed. What can be described without qualification is the patterning itself: repeated roots, clustered consonants, rhyming endings.[25]

      The poetry of chapters 32–33 is more densely patterned than the surrounding prose. The opening of Moses’ song summons heaven and earth with va-adaberah (“that I may speak”) and develops four similes linked by repeated comparative forms:

      הַאֲזִ֥ינוּ הַשָּׁמַ֖יִם וַאֲדַבֵּ֑רָה וְתִשְׁמַ֥ע הָאָ֖רֶץ אִמְרֵי־פִֽי׃ יַעֲרֹ֤ף כַּמָּטָר֙ לִקְחִ֔י תִּזַּ֥ל כַּטַּ֖ל אִמְרָתִ֑י כִּשְׂעִירִ֣ם עֲלֵי־דֶ֔שֶׁא וְכִרְבִיבִ֖ים עֲלֵי־עֵֽשֶׂב׃ Ha’azinu ha-shamayim va-adaberah, ve-tishma ha-aretz imrei fi. Ya‘arof ka-matar likchi, tizal ka-tal imrati, ki-se‘irim alei deshe, ve-khirvivim alei esev. “Give ear, O heavens, and let me speak; let the earth hear the words of my mouth. May my teaching drop like rain, my speech distil like dew, like showers on new grass, like droplets on the herb.” Deuteronomy 32:1–2 (emphasis added)

      Other examples include the three rhyming forms shamanta avita kasita (“you grew fat, you grew thick, you were gorged,” 32:15) and the triplet le-shamah le-mashal ve-li-shninah (“a horror, a proverb, and a byword,” 28:37). Repetition gives tzedek tzedek tirdof (“justice, justice shall you pursue,” 16:20) its compact force. Arami oved avi (26:5) begins three successive words with aleph, while nachash saraf ve-akrav ve-tzimma’on (“serpent, fiery serpent, scorpion, and thirst,” 8:15) accumulates marked consonants. Comparable devices occur elsewhere in biblical prose and poetry; these examples do not establish a feature unique to Deuteronomy.

      Sound and script also interact in the received scribal tradition. In a Torah scroll, the ayin of shema and the dalet of echad in Deuteronomy 6:4 are enlarged; read together they spell ed (עד), “witness.” The device is visual rather than audible, and it belongs to the scribes rather than to the composition, but it shows the same appetite for meaning carried in letters that the bee reading depends on.

      Deborah and related bee imagery

      The name Deborah (Devorah, דבורה), borne by the prophetess and judge in the Book of Judges, literally means “bee.” This lexical connection makes her relevant to the history of bee imagery in Jewish interpretation, but Judges does not connect her name with the title of Deuteronomy. [26]

      Judges 4–5 presents Deborah as a prophet and premonarchic leader who adjudicates disputes beneath a palm in the hill country of Ephraim and summons Barak to fight Sisera. The prose account and the Song of Deborah depict authority distributed among a prophet, a military commander, allied tribes, and Jael, who kills Sisera. Many scholars regard portions of the song as early Hebrew poetry, although its date and compositional history remain debated.

      Rabbinic tradition is divided about her. The Bavli at Megillah 14a counts her among seven prophetesses of Israel—Sarah, Miriam, Deborah, Hannah, Abigail, Huldah and Esther—while Pesachim 66b reads her song critically, taking the shift from “until I, Deborah, arose” (Judges 5:7) to the imperative “awake, awake, Deborah” (Judges 5:12) as evidence that prophecy withdraws from one who becomes self-important. [27]

      Her judicial role generated sustained halakhic discussion, since Maimonides rules that public appointments are restricted to men (Mishneh Torah, Kings 1:5). Tosafot and later authorities address the case of a woman who plainly “judged Israel,” proposing variously that Deborah taught the law rather than adjudicating, that the people accepted her authority voluntarily, or that prophecy constitutes its own warrant; Radak and Ralbag on Judges 4:4–5 emphasize her prophetic standing alongside her arbitration beneath the palm. The phrase “palm of Deborah” in Judges 4:5 later supplied the title of Moses Cordovero’s ethical work Tomer Devorah. The book concerns imitation of divine attributes; its title does not make it a commentary on Deborah or on the devarim–devorim wordplay. [28]

      Kabbalistic and Hasidic reception

      Kabbalistic literature gives speech a central place in its account of creation and revelation, and modern readers have brought that theology to bear on Deuteronomy. None of the sources described here comments on the bee comparison or on the spelling of 1:44; the application is a recent one, and worth keeping apart from the medieval material it draws on.

      In sefirotic symbolism articulated speech (dibbur) is associated with Malkhut and the Shekhinah: speech is the outward disclosure of what remains hidden as thought or voice. Terminology varies between kabbalistic schools, but the association of the lowest sefirah with utterance is standard.[29]

      Zohar III 7a takes up “Moses would speak, and God would answer him with a voice” (Exodus 19:19) together with the Talmudic statement that the Deuteronomic curses came “from Moses’ own mouth,” and concludes that no word of Torah came from Moses alone: the voice he spoke with was joined to divine power. Later writers compress this into the formula that the Shekhinah spoke from Moses’ throat, which is a summary rather than a quotation.[30]

      A separate strand of homily concerns the wilderness. Sefer Yetzirah and the sources that follow it treat the twenty-two Hebrew letters as instruments of creation, and Hasidic teaching uses the resemblance of midbar to medabber, often with Hosea 2:16, to make the wilderness the place where speech can be heard. Attributions vary between anthologies, and the link is homiletic rather than etymological.[31]

      Tanya, Sha’ar ha-Yichud veha-Emunah, chapter 1, teaches that the letters of divine speech sustain creation from moment to moment, so that Hebrew words and their combinations carry weight in Chabad thought that they would not carry as mere vocabulary. It is easy to see why a modern reader would extend that doctrine to two words that share their consonants, and equally clear that Shneur Zalman does not.[32]

      Modern parallel: the English “spelling bee”

      The English phrase spelling bee also joins language and bees, but it is unrelated to the Hebrew terms. In this English usage, bee means a gathering for shared work or competition, as in quilting bee. Dictionaries commonly derive that sense, though not with complete certainty, from dialectal been or bean, “voluntary help given by neighbours,” ultimately from Middle English bene, “prayer,” “boon,” or a required service.

      On this account, association with the insect influenced the spelling and interpretation of the communal term. “Spelling match” is attested from 1808 and “spelling bee” by the middle of the nineteenth century. Noah Webster’s spelling books helped make such contests a feature of American education.[33]

      The two cases are unrelated, and they are not even parallel in kind. If the dialectal derivation holds, English speakers came to hear an insect in a word that never contained one, and the older sense was lost in the process. Devarim Rabbah sets its two words side by side on purpose and says as much. Only one of them is a mistake.

      Assessment

      Two points are firm. Devarim Rabbah 1:6 really does read the opening of Deuteronomy through the bee, and can be cited at a fixed place rather than described as a tradition in general circulation. The consonantal identity of “the words” in 1:1 and “the bees” in 1:44 is a genuine feature of the Masoretic text, not an artefact of loose transliteration. Most of what has been built on those two facts is looser. The midrash is a homily on a resemblance, and the resemblance is not an etymology; the spelling is an opening the midrash could exploit, not a signature of design; the mystical and Hasidic material addresses speech, letters and the standing of Moses’ voice, and reaches Deuteronomy 1:44 only when a modern reader takes it there.

      The weakest claims in circulation are the comparative ones. Deuteronomy is the most sustained piece of persuasion in the Torah, which is not the same as being its most complex Hebrew, and impressions of difficulty in recitation depend on a reading tradition that postdates the text by centuries. Nothing here settles who wrote the book or when, and the wordplay does not require an answer: it is a fact about how the book has been read, and it has been read that way for at least a millennium and a half.
