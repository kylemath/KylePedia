    The register shift in Deuteronomy

    Entry Sources Tags
    Read Collection Contents
    The register shift in Deuteronomy
    Corpus Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible: Westminster Leningrad Codex text with morphological tagging
    Span counted Genesis 1:1 – Deuteronomy 34:12, 79,985 words
    Distinct dictionary roots (Torah) 3,535
    New roots in Deuteronomy 330 (9.3% of the Torah total)
    New spellings in Deuteronomy 1,188 (14.3% of the Torah total)
    Central finding Second-person finite verbs: 15–26% of verbs in Genesis–Numbers, 37% in Deuteronomy

    Readers moving from Genesis through Numbers into Deuteronomy often report that the Hebrew starts to feel harder: more academic, more poetic, full of look-alike endings that are easy to confuse. A count of every one of the 79,985 words in the Five Books shows that this impression is not explained by a larger vocabulary. Deuteronomy introduces roughly the number of new dictionary roots that the growth curve of the first four books already predicted, and somewhat fewer. What changes is whom the text addresses and in what grammatical person, and that change forces roots the reader already knows into unfamiliar-looking shapes far more often than in the earlier books.

    Two separate effects are responsible, and they are worth telling apart. The larger one is a shift in address: known roots are reused as second-person speech — commands, obligations, and pronominal “you” and “your” endings — at two to three times their earlier rate, producing chains of near-identical endings on familiar words. The smaller one is a genuine increase in rare and previously unattested vocabulary, concentrated in legal and covenantal terms rather than narrative ones. Both are demonstrable by direct count rather than by impression, and this entry gives the counts.

      Method and corpus

      The counts in this entry come from every word of Genesis through Deuteronomy in the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible project’s edition of the Westminster Leningrad Codex, which attaches a morphological tag to each word recording its part of speech, verb stem, conjugation, person, gender, number, and any attached prefix or suffix.[1] Each word was split into three layers: a prefix-stripped form, in which the inseparable particles vav, he, lamed, bet, kaf, mem, and shin are removed but conjugation, gender, number, and any pronominal suffix are kept; a root, the Strong’s-numbered dictionary entry the project’s editors assigned to that stem; and the full grammatical tag itself, which supplies the conjugation and person labels used throughout this entry.[2] On this scheme shamar, shamarta, tishmeru, and lishmor — “he kept,” “you kept,” “you [plural] shall keep,” “to keep” — are four different forms of one root.

      A form or root counts as new the first time it appears in reading order, from Genesis 1:1 through Deuteronomy 34:12; every later occurrence of the same item is a repeat. Across the Five Books this yields 79,985 words, 8,320 distinct prefix-stripped forms, and 3,535 distinct roots.

      The vocabulary is not larger than expected

      Vocabulary growth in any sufficiently long text follows a predictable curve: the count of distinct items rises quickly at first and flattens as more text accumulates, because most of what a later chapter needs has already been said earlier. Fitting that curve to the growth of Genesis through Numbers and extending it across a stretch of text the length of Deuteronomy gives an expectation of about 1,099 new prefix-stripped forms and about 407 new roots.[3] Deuteronomy supplies 1,188 new forms — 14.3% of the Torah’s 8,320 — which is 8% above that expectation. It supplies 330 new roots — 9.3% of the Torah’s 3,535 — which is 19% below expectation. Numbers alone, a book of comparable length, introduces more new roots (451) than Deuteronomy does.

      By the ordinary meaning of vocabulary — the stock of distinct dictionary words a book draws on — Deuteronomy is not exceptional. Whatever a reader is responding to when the Hebrew starts to feel harder has to be something other than a bigger dictionary.

      From narration to address

      Biblical Hebrew’s finite verb system does not mark past, present, and future the way English does. It marks something closer to completed versus developing action, and each of those is further split into an ordinary and a narrative-sequential form, giving grammarians four labels: qatal (the “perfect,” a simple completed action), wayyiqtol (the “sequential imperfect,” the backbone of Hebrew narrative — “and he did,” “and he said,” “and he went”), yiqtol (the “imperfect,” a developing or habitual action), and weqatal (the “sequential perfect,” which in legal and hortatory prose often carries a future or obligatory sense, “and you shall…”).[4] None of the four corresponds to an English past perfect, and Biblical Hebrew has no dedicated past-perfect form at all.

      What moves sharply across the Five Books is not the balance of completed against developing action but the balance of wayyiqtol against everything else. In Genesis, wayyiqtol accounts for 41.6% of every finite verb — the narrative chain running almost uninterrupted. That share falls to 23.6% in Exodus, 7.5% in Leviticus, rises back to 23.7% in Numbers, and falls to 7.2% in Deuteronomy, lower than in every book but Leviticus. Qatal stays roughly level across all five books (15–19%); yiqtol and weqatal together rise from 14% of Genesis’s verbs to 45% of Deuteronomy’s. The book is not more retrospective. It is less narrated.

      Alongside conjugation, grammatical person shifts even more sharply. In Genesis, Exodus, and Numbers, 65–77% of finite verbs are third person — “he did,” “she said,” “they went.” In Deuteronomy, third person falls to 53% and second person rises to 37%, higher than in any other book by eleven percentage points. The combination that matters most is second-person yiqtol or weqatal — the closest Hebrew comes to an obligation, “you shall keep,” “you shall love”: 20% of Deuteronomy’s verbs carry this combination, against 3% in Genesis and 8–13% in Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers. Pronominal suffixes move the same way: 20.3% of all Deuteronomy’s words carry an attached pronoun, the highest of the five books, and 54% of those suffixes mean “you” or “your,” against 18–24% in the earlier books.[5]

      The Shema and the verses following it put every one of these features into six verses of continuous prose:

      שְׁמַ֖ע יִשְׂרָאֵ֑ל יְהוָ֥ה אֱלֹהֵ֖ינוּ יְהוָ֥ה אֶחָֽד׃ וְאָ֣הַבְתָּ֔ אֵ֖ת יְהוָ֣ה אֱלֹהֶ֑יךָ בְּכָל־לְבָבְךָ֥ וּבְכָל־נַפְשְׁךָ֖ וּבְכָל־מְאֹדֶֽךָ׃ וְהָי֞וּ הַדְּבָרִ֣ים הָאֵ֗לֶּה אֲשֶׁ֨ר אָנֹכִ֧י מְצַוְּךָ֛ הַיּ֖וֹם עַל־לְבָבֶֽךָ׃ וְשִׁנַּנְתָּ֣ם לְבָנֶ֔יךָ וְדִבַּרְתָּ֖ בָּ֑ם בְּשִׁבְתְּךָ֤ בְּבֵיתֶ֨ךָ֙ וּבְלֶכְתְּךָ֣ בַדֶּ֔רֶךְ וּֽבְשָׁכְבְּךָ֖ וּבְקוּמֶֽךָ׃ Shema Yisrael, Adonai Eloheinu, Adonai echad. Ve-ahavta et Adonai Elohekha be-khol levavkha u-ve-khol nafshekha u-ve-khol me’odekha. Ve-hayu ha-devarim ha-eleh asher anokhi metzavvekha ha-yom al levavekha. Ve-shinnantam le-vanekha ve-dibbarta bam, be-shivtekha be-veitekha u-ve-lekhtekha va-derekh, u-ve-shokhbekha u-ve-kumekha. “Hear, O Israel: the LORD is our God, the LORD is one. You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might. And these words that I command you today shall be upon your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your children, and speak of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk on the road, and when you lie down, and when you rise.” Deuteronomy 6:4–7

      Every finite verb in this passage is second-person singular. Four different verb roots — sit, walk, lie down, rise — are each cast as an infinitive plus the identical -kha (“your” / “when you”) ending: be-shivtekha, u-ve-lekhtekha, u-ve-shokhbekha, u-ve-kumekha. The word for “heart,” levav rather than the shorter lev familiar from Genesis, carries the same -kha suffix twice more. None of these seven roots is new to the Torah; every ending on them is doing the work of second-person address, four times in six verses.[6]

      Old roots in new grammatical clothing

      Of Deuteronomy’s 1,188 debuting forms, 78.5% belong to roots the Torah already knew — the highest share of any book (55.1% in Genesis, 67.7% in Exodus, 72.7% in Leviticus, 67.0% in Numbers). A form can be “new” in this count while its root is one of the most familiar words in the Torah. The root yarash, “to take possession of” or “inherit,” had already appeared 30 times since its first use at Genesis 15:3; Deuteronomy is nonetheless the first place either of its imperative spellings, resh or reshu (“take possession!”), occurs. The same is true of the paragogic nun: an archaic-sounding extra -un or -un ending added to a second-person plural imperfect verb, so that tishme’un stands beside the ordinary tishme’u, both meaning “you [plural] shall hear.”[7] This ending occurs roughly four times per thousand words in Deuteronomy, two to seven times the rate of the earlier books, always attached to roots the reader already knows.

      It is worth stating precisely what does not increase. Measuring how often one word in the text differs from the very next word by exactly one consonant, Deuteronomy has the lowest rate of the five books — 4.9 instances per thousand words, against 7.1 in Genesis. Literal side-by-side near-rhyme is not what is happening. What produces the sense of “similar but different” pairs is the same three consonants recurring across a sentence or two in different grammatical dress, not sitting adjacent. Deuteronomy 16:18 supplies a clean instance:

      שֹׁפְטִ֣ים וְשֹֽׁטְרִ֗ים תִּֽתֶּן־לְךָ֙ בְּכָל־שְׁעָרֶ֔יךָ אֲשֶׁ֨ר יְהוָ֧ה אֱלֹהֶ֛יךָ נֹתֵ֥ן לְךָ֖ לִשְׁבָטֶ֑יךָ וְשָׁפְט֥וּ אֶת־הָעָ֖ם מִשְׁפַּט־צֶֽדֶק׃ Shofetim ve-shoterim titten lekha be-khol she’arekha asher Adonai Elohekha noten lekha li-shvatekha, ve-shafetu et ha-am mishpat tzedek. “Judges and officers you shall appoint for yourself in all your gates, which the LORD your God is giving you, tribe by tribe, and they shall judge the people with righteous judgment.” Deuteronomy 16:18

      Shofetim (“judges,” a noun) and ve-shafetu (“and they shall judge,” a verb) share the same three root consonants, shin-pe-tet, and differ mainly in their vowel pattern and grammatical function — four words apart in one sentence, not adjacent, and both from a root already familiar. This, rather than adjacent rhyme, is the shape the “difficult pairs” take.

      The words that are genuinely new

      Not all of the effect is repackaging of old roots. About one root in five among Deuteronomy’s 330 new roots occurs five times or fewer anywhere in the Hebrew Bible — the rate at which Leviticus and Numbers, not Genesis, introduce vocabulary. At this level Deuteronomy resembles the earlier legal material rather than the earlier narrative. A concrete case: ma’akeh, the parapet a roof must have under Deuteronomy 22:8, occurs exactly once in the entire Hebrew Bible.[8]

      A more striking case is the root lamad, “to teach” or “to learn.” It does not occur anywhere in Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, or Numbers. Its first occurrence in the Hebrew Bible is Deuteronomy 4:1 — asher anokhi melammed etkhem, “which I am teaching you” — after which it occurs sixteen more times within Deuteronomy and another sixty-eight times across the rest of the Bible. Along with it come a cluster of legal and covenantal abstractions rather than narrative nouns: choq (“statute”), mishpat (“judgment,” “ordinance”), to’evah (“abomination”), bachar (“to choose”), and the purpose and warning particles lema’an (“in order that”) and pen (“lest”), all of which recur through Deuteronomy’s legal sections at a density the earlier books do not reach.[9]

      But most of the words a reader is likely to notice as suddenly frequent are not new at all — they were already in the text and are simply spoken far more often, which is the register effect of the previous two sections wearing a lexical face rather than a grammatical one. Shamar, “to keep” or “to guard,” occurs 73 times in Deuteronomy alone, nearly matching its combined total across Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers (15 + 25 + 16 + 19 = 75 times). The heavier form of “heart,” levav, is used only eight times across those same four books combined (3 + 1 + 3 + 1) and forty-seven times in Deuteronomy alone, while the shorter lev, common in Genesis and Exodus, nearly disappears. Bachar, “to choose,” goes from eight combined uses in the first four books to thirty-one in Deuteronomy; to’evah, “abomination,” from ten combined uses (mostly in Leviticus) to seventeen. None of these four roots is new. Each is simply spoken at two to eight times its earlier rate.

      Scholarly notes

      Form counts approximate spelling, not every nuance of sound. A prefix-stripped “form” in this analysis is defined at the level of consonantal stem, suffix, and grammatical tag that the OSHB editors assigned; it does not separately track every vowel-pattern distinction a trained reader might hear as meaningfully different. The counts describe the corpus’s written skeleton rather than every phonetic nuance of the vocalized text.

      “Tense” is a borrowed label, not a claim about Hebrew grammar. Qatal, wayyiqtol, yiqtol, and weqatal do not map onto English past, present, and future, and Biblical Hebrew has no dedicated past-perfect form. Describing Deuteronomy’s shift using tense language translates a learner’s felt experience into the nearest available grammatical categories; it is not a claim that Hebrew marks time the way English does.

      The poetry of chapters 32–33 is excluded from these averages. The Song of Moses and the blessing of the tribes have a denser and rarer vocabulary profile than the surrounding legal and hortatory prose. They were not separated out in the book-level figures above, so a reader’s sense of unusual difficulty in those two chapters draws on real archaic vocabulary that the whole-book averages partly dilute rather than capture.

      The lemma and morphological tags are the source project’s editorial judgments. They were not independently re-checked word by word for this analysis. Disagreements among grammarians on individual forms — a disputed root division, a contested paragogic ending — would shift the exact percentages given here without changing the overall pattern: a fall in narrative wayyiqtol, a rise in second-person address, and a small, legally concentrated core of genuinely new vocabulary.
